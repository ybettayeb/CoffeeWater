# Bicarbonate, not magnesium, steers the cup

Of all the water variables, only alkalinity (bicarbonate, "KH") has controlled, peer-reviewed sensory evidence behind it. In a 2026 University of Copenhagen study, adding bicarbonate to ultrapure water at up to 300 mg/L **"substantially reduced perceived acidity, fruitiness, and complexity, while increasing roasted and bitter notes"**. Hardness alone **"produced no marked flavor changes"**, even when calcium was added above typical drinking-water levels ([KU research portal](https://researchprofiles.ku.dk/en/publications/impact-of-water-composition-on-coffee-sensory-properties-a-compre/)). A 2024 chemistry study agrees: at drinking-water concentrations, calcium and magnesium barely changed how much of the main organic acids ended up in the brew ([Heliyon via ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2405844024026562)). The popular rule that "magnesium brings out fruit and florals, calcium adds body" therefore rests on a 2014 computer-modelling paper, small practitioner cuppings and marketing, not on measured flavour. The other variables have weaker support:
- **Sodium** has thin but consistent evidence that it reduces a "clean" taste.
- **Chloride and sulfate** have flavour roles borrowed from beer brewing; no coffee study tests them.
- **Water pH** adds little once alkalinity is known.
- **Espresso** is governed more by machine limits than by flavour science: chloride ≤ 30 mg/L, alkalinity about 40–80 mg/L as CaCO₃ and hardness about 70–100 mg/L as CaCO₃ on La Marzocco's specification.

Among the commercial concentrates, APAX Lab declares its salts but publishes no ion amounts. The only number it gives is about 30 ppm on a TDS meter per gram of concentrate per litre, so the site cannot predict APAX ion profiles. Lotus is the only brand whose ion additions can be calculated exactly, and every brand's flavour claims are marketing. For the site's flavour model v1, the evidence supports the core choice of making KH the main driver. It does not support the magnesium-share and calcium weights, the sulfate effects, the hardness-driven fruit, body and chocolate terms, or the "thin" and "chalky" warnings, and in places it contradicts them.

> **Read this first: what this report could and could not check.** Every research pass behind this report hit DNS or proxy failures on publisher and manufacturer sites (ACS, PMC, ScienceDirect, Wiley, J-STAGE, DergiPark, DiVA, ZHAW, SCA, BWT, Royal Coffee, Daily Coffee News, Barista Hustle, apaxlab.com and others). **Almost every finding here comes from abstracts and search-engine summaries of the cited pages, not from full texts.** That has three consequences:
> - Effect sizes, panel sizes, concentration grids and statistics for the key studies are missing.
> - Several numbers come from a single summary.
> - Some secondary accounts contradict each other.
>
> The citations point to the primary pages so they can be checked. Every number that carries weight in the site's model is listed in the re-verification table near the end. None of those numbers should be published until someone has read the primary text.

## Grading the evidence before using it

The model needs to know what kind of evidence sits behind each claim. This report uses four grades:
- **A**: controlled experiment, peer reviewed, with sensory or chemical measurement.
- **B**: peer reviewed but indirect. This covers computational models, reviews, chemistry without tasting, and conference papers.
- **C**: structured industry panels, standards bodies and manufacturer specifications.
- **D**: blogs, forums, retailer copy and marketing.

The grades matter because the coffee-water field has a strong consensus that rests on weak foundations. The specialty-coffee standards are themselves grade C. They are expert consensus with equipment-protection input, and no published sensory panel underlies them. Wellinger, Smrke and Yeretzian of the ZHAW Coffee Excellence Center, who co-developed the SCAE Water Chart, wrote that "the existence of a single optimum value for total hardness and alkalinity is most probably unlikely" ([Wellinger et al. 2017](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/312183310_Water_for_Extraction-Composition_Recommendations_and_Treatment)). The 2024 Umeå authors put it more bluntly: industry water recommendations "still lack a scientific basis" ([DOAJ abstract](https://doaj.org/article/32b12f7ec2a54c689511ea241d4a4a37)).

The table summarises where each variable stands.

| Variable | Best-supported effect on flavour | Best grade | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alkalinity / bicarbonate (KH) | More KH: less acidity, fruit and complexity; more roast and bitterness; flatter | A (Copenhagen 2026; Pangborn 1971) | Established in direction; dose-response shape unknown |
| Calcium hardness | No marked effect at realistic levels | A (Copenhagen 2026) | Established null at realistic levels |
| Magnesium vs calcium | Panels conflict: Mg "sweet/floral" (Crown), Ca-rich "more bitter/astringent" (ZHAW/BWT), Mg correlates with bitterness (Korean 2017) | C, contradictory | Unresolved |
| Extraction yield vs Ca/Mg | Acid extraction largely independent of Ca/Mg at drinking-water levels | A (Bratthäll 2024) | Contradicts the "Mg extracts more" claim |
| Total minerals / TDS | Consumer liking falls as TDS rises (0 → 100 → 200 ppm), at least for medium and dark roasts | A (consumer panels; confounded with alkalinity) | Probable, but cause unclear |
| Sodium | Lower "clean taste"; slightly salty at very high doses | A− (Imamura 2024, n≈6); A-dated (Pangborn) | Direction plausible; threshold unknown |
| Potassium | No coffee study found | D | Unsupported |
| Chloride vs sulfate | Beer heuristic only; Five Senses saw little effect at typical levels | D | Unsupported in coffee |
| pH of brewing water | Not independent of alkalinity; taste unaffected from pH 5.5 to 9.1 | B (Fibrianto 2018), C (ZHAW) | Do not model as a flavour driver |
| Roast level × water | Low hardness suits light roasts, high hardness suits dark roasts (Liu 2025) vs purified water best liked for medium/dark roasts (Kang 2022) | A, contradictory | Unresolved |

## Alkalinity is the only lever with controlled proof

The Copenhagen study is the cleanest test available (Rune, Steen, Kjærgaard, Maroun, Bredie and Kjaergaard, *Journal of Food Science* 91(9), e71434, 2026). It brewed a reference coffee in ultrapure water and compared it with brews in ultrapure water plus calcium, magnesium or bicarbonate, alone or combined. The levels ran "from the lower levels found within the 'ideal brewing zone' up to higher experimental extremes". Raising bicarbonate up to **300 mg/L HCO₃⁻** (≈ 246 mg/L as CaCO₃, ≈ 14 °dKH) cut acidity, fruitiness and complexity and raised roasted and bitter notes. The authors concluded that **"alkalinity, not hardness, was the primary determinant of sensory changes"**. They also concluded that "variations in calcium within realistic ranges showed little sensory relevance" ([KU research portal](https://researchprofiles.ku.dk/en/publications/impact-of-water-composition-on-coffee-sensory-properties-a-compre/)).

One detail matters for any model that weights calcium. The small differences seen in samples with both calcium and bicarbonate were attributed to **calcium carbonate precipitation lowering the effective bicarbonate**, "rather than to any direct flavor contribution from calcium" (same source). The abstract does not give panel size, intermediate doses or effect sizes. Secondary accounts say a single Colombian coffee and duo-trio screening were used ([QxMD listing](https://read.qxmd.com/read/42746805/impact-of-water-composition-on-coffee-sensory-properties-a-comprehensive-analysis)). So the study shows direction and an upper anchor. It cannot say whether 50 or 100 mg/L HCO₃⁻ already tastes different from pure water.

Older work points the same way at cruder doses. Pangborn, Trabue and Little (1971) made coffee from distilled water spiked with about 750 ppm of single minerals. Carbonate solutions gave the least desirable drinks, described as "flat and insipid" ([Wiley abstract](https://ift.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1365-2621.1971.tb04061.x)). Secondary accounts add that sodium bicarbonate gave the most bitter coffee, and that "the carbonates were the worst offenders, resulting in a bitter, flat coffee" ([ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/229783358_Analysis_of_coffee_tea_and_artificially_flavored_drinks_prepared_from_mineralized_waters)). Practitioner panels agree. Royal Coffee's Crown lab found that "high alkalinity water resulted in very flat, chalky, coffee with muted acidity and muddled flavors" ([Daily Coffee News, Part II](https://dailycoffeenews.com/2018/08/29/a-practical-water-guide-for-coffee-professionals-part-ii-the-sensory-data/)). This is the most consistent finding across all four evidence grades.

The mechanism explains why alkalinity, rather than pH or hardness, is the right predictor. Bicarbonate neutralises coffee acids after they are extracted (HCO₃⁻ + H⁺ → CO₂ + H₂O). It does not change which acids are extracted. In drip coffee, perceived sourness tracks titratable acidity strongly and pH only weakly ([ACS Food Sci. Technol. 2020](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/abs/10.1021/acsfoodscitech.0c00078)). A cold-brew study likewise found "little correlation between the pH and TA" in hot coffee ([PMC6207714](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6207714/)). Bicarbonate consumes titratable acid, so it reduces sourness directly.

Water pH carries almost no buffering mass, and the coffee's own acids overwhelm it. An Indonesian pour-over study found "taste and flavour attributes were not affected by the pH of brewing water within the range of 5.5 to 9.1" ([IOP Conf. Ser. 2018](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1755-1315/102/1/012095)). ZHAW's Marco Wellinger states that "it is far more important to keep alkalinity within a certain range than pH" ([SCA 25 Magazine](https://sca.coffee/sca-news/25/issue-9/english/water-and-coffee-acidity-how-to-adapt-your-water-for-different-extraction-methods-25-magazine-issue-9-pxjby)).

The descriptor "bitter" under high alkalinity probably combines two effects. Bitterness may truly rise, but suppressing acidity also unmasks bitterness and roast character that the acidity had been balancing. The Copenhagen wording, "roasted and bitter notes", fits either reading. This matters for the site's "chocolate & roast" dimension. The evidence shows that alkalinity makes roast and bitterness more dominant. It does not show that alkalinity adds cocoa or hazelnut flavour.

Two practical consequences follow for a bottled-water site. First, **bicarbonate is the column to rank waters on**. Mineral waters with several hundred mg/L of bicarbonate sit at or above the Copenhagen study's "substantial effect" ceiling. Second, **no concentrate can remove bicarbonate**. The only way to lower a water's KH is to dilute it with a low-mineral water. Mixing is linear by volume: c_mix = (V₁·c₁ + V₂·c₂)/(V₁ + V₂). Royal Coffee published worked examples of blending waters of different TDS ([Royal Coffee](https://royalcoffee.com/blending-water-some-important-math-to-help-you-mix-waters-of-different-tds/)).

## Calcium and magnesium matter far less than the lore claims

The "magnesium extracts more" idea traces back to Hendon, Colonna-Dashwood and Colonna-Dashwood (2014, *J. Agric. Food Chem.* 62:4947). That paper used density functional theory to compute how strongly Na⁺, Mg²⁺ and Ca²⁺ bind five coffee acids, caffeine and eugenol. **No coffee was brewed, analysed or tasted** ([University of Bath portal](https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/the-role-of-dissolved-cations-in-coffee-extraction/)). The model ranked binding strength as Mg > Ca > Na. The flavour claims came afterwards. In the press release Hendon said "high magnesium ion levels increase the extraction of coffee into water and improve the taste" ([Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2014-06-science-coffee.html)), and the follow-up book *Water for Coffee* (2015) built on that. Even sympathetic readers noted that "the model doesn't predict big differences in the flavour of the brew, so much as in the overall extraction" ([Coffee ad Astra](https://coffeeadastra.com/2018/12/16/water-for-coffee-extraction/)).

Binding energy is not the same thing as extraction yield. By my estimate, the water's cations are present at around 0.5–2 mmol/L, while the brew carries tens of millimoles of coffee solutes, and the coffee bed releases its own potassium, magnesium and calcium.

The first direct experimental test did not support the mechanism. Bratthäll, Figueira and Nording (*Heliyon* 2024) added MgCl₂ or CaCl₂ either before or after brewing. They then measured citric, malic, lactic and quinic acid by GC-MS and NMR. **"At concentrations typically found in drinking water, the salts resulted in limited variation of the acid content, while ten-fold higher salt concentrations produced more pronounced variations."** Pre-brew and post-brew additions gave similar results, so the authors concluded that "extraction of acids proceeds independent of the water composition" in most cases. They allowed that "interactions taking place post-brew may, however, influence the perceived flavor" ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2405844024026562); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38434259/)). Bratthäll's earlier Umeå thesis came to the same view: cations "affect perception rather than extraction" ([DiVA](https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:1546046/FULLTEXT01.pdf)).

An informal Australian refractometer test points the same way. It spiked RO water with MgCl₂ at up to 500 ppm and found no statistically significant change in brew TDS. The author concluded that pouring technique mattered more than magnesium ([Five Senses](https://fivesenses.com.au/blogs/news/experimenting-with-the-effect-of-water-quality-on-coffee)).

Taste thresholds show why realistic hardness barely registers. Bratthäll et al. cite perception thresholds in coffee of **300 ppm Ca²⁺ and 200 ppm Mg²⁺**, "typically not reached in tap water" ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2405844024026562)). The original source of those figures was not identified. In plain water, the WHO puts the calcium taste threshold at about 100–300 mg/L, depending on the anion ([WHO GDWQ ch.10](https://cdn.who.int/media/docs/default-source/wash-documents/water-safety-and-quality/dwq-guidelines-4/gdwq4-with-add1-chap10.pdf)). Magnesium thresholds in water conflict:
- WHO says only that it is lower than calcium's.
- Health Canada gives 100–500 mg/L ([Health Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/programs/guidelines-canadian-drinking-water-quality-operational-parameters.html)).
- A European mineral-water paper lists 45–60 mg/L ([Foods 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7765973/)).

By my arithmetic, the SCA hardness range of 17–85 mg/L as CaCO₃ equals about 7–34 mg/L of calcium, or about 4–21 mg/L of magnesium. That is roughly an order of magnitude below the cited coffee thresholds.

The practitioner panels that gave rise to the Mg/Ca lore contradict one another:
- **Crown lab (Royal Coffee).** Its small internal panel found magnesium gave "lots of sweet and floral attributes, particularly in lighter roasts". As roasts darkened, tasters found "the mouthfeel overwhelming, and sweetness cloying". The same panel **preferred the calcium-based formula overall** on light-roast washed coffees, and calcium water tasted "more acidic" ([Royal Coffee, Part II](https://royalcoffee.com/treading-water-a-practical-h2o-guide-for-coffee-professionals-part-ii-the-sensory-data/)).
- **ZHAW/BWT.** Three trained cuppers tasted blind, in triplicate, at a fixed hardness of 70 and alkalinity of 40 mg/L as CaCO₃. They found a calcium-rich 1:3 Mg:Ca ratio significantly more bitter and astringent, with slightly less fruit ([BWT Magnesium Technology](https://www.bwt-wam.com/en/technologies/bwt-magnesium-technology/)). BWT sells magnesium cartridges, and no peer-reviewed version of this work was found.
- **Korean trained panel (2017).** A quantitative descriptive panel on commercial waters found **magnesium correlated most strongly with bitterness**, and bitterness ran opposite to acidity and sweetness ([KoreaScience](https://koreascience.kr/article/JAKO201721242143947.page)).
- **SCA partner blind test.** Fourteen blind testers did not recognise the "theoretically better extraction" of magnesium-rich water ([SCA News](https://sca.coffee/sca-news/news-from-our-partners/water-and-the-taste-of-coffee-ay9gh)).
- **Barista Hustle.** Its in-house magnesium cupping had two unblinded tasters, who knew the hypothesis ([Barista Hustle TWC 5.04](https://www.baristahustle.com/lesson/twc-5-04-magnesium-cupping/)).

Three sets of results deserve attention because they cut against the expert consensus.

**Consumers prefer purified water.** Kang, Piao and Ko (2022) had 167 consumers rate coffee made with water at about 0, 100 and 200 ppm TDS. **Liking fell as TDS rose at medium and dark roasts**, and purified water was preferred ([Food Quality and Preference](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0950329322001069)). In a Korean student panel of 103, distilled-water coffee was the most preferred, and bitterness, acidity and body did not differ significantly across the waters ([KNU](https://research.knu.ac.kr/en/publications/effect-of-water-hardness-on-coffee-composition-and-coffee-prefere/)). In the Korean trained-panel study, liking fell as total mineral content rose ([KoreaScience](https://koreascience.kr/article/JAKO201721242143947.page)).

**Experts disagree about distilled water.** The Crown's professional cuppers gave distilled water **the highest tallied score** in their second round ([Daily Coffee News, Part II](https://dailycoffeenews.com/2018/08/29/a-practical-water-guide-for-coffee-professionals-part-ii-the-sensory-data/)). Pangborn's 1971 panel called distilled-water coffee "excessively sour" ([Wiley abstract](https://ift.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1365-2621.1971.tb04061.x)). Forum blind tastings found it "thin and harsh" ([Home-Barista](https://www.home-barista.com/water/royal-coffee-blind-tasting-waters-t54215.html)).

**Hardness and roast results conflict.** One 2025 filter study found that "water samples of high/low hardness values benefited the extraction of taste compounds and the exhibition of secondary dominant tastes for coffees of dark/light roasting degrees, respectively" ([Liu et al. 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0889157525008026)). That points in the opposite direction from Kang's consumer data on dark roasts. Liu's alkalinity values were not visible, so its "hardness" effect may partly be an alkalinity effect.

Natural- and bottled-water studies show plenty of water effects, but hardness in them rises together with bicarbonate, sodium and TDS. A Turkish bottled-water study found that the water richest in calcium and bicarbonate gave the highest extraction yield but not the best taste. It found that high Na/Cl reduced aroma and balance ([DergiPark](https://dergipark.org.tr/tr/download/article-file/4984487)). For Turkish coffee, hard water gave the most bitterness and soft water the most sweetness and sourness ([Gıda](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/pub/gida/article/938427)). These are real-world signals. They cannot separate calcium from carbonate, and the Copenhagen design, which can, found that hardness on its own did almost nothing.

## Sodium, chloride, sulfate and potassium rest on thin or borrowed evidence

**Sodium** has the best support of the minor ions, though the support is still thin. Imamura (2024) found that low-sodium water from melted ice gave coffee a cleaner taste. Adding sodium back raised taste-sensor saltiness and lowered "clean taste" ([J-STAGE](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/fstr/30/2/30_FSTR-D-23-00129/_html/-char/en)). The human panel reportedly had six people, and the author works for an ice company ([Gota review](https://gota.cafe/en/books/sca-water-quality-handbook)). Pangborn's sodium chloride water, at roughly 750 ppm, made coffee only "slightly salty" ([ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/229783358_Analysis_of_coffee_tea_and_artificially_flavored_drinks_prepared_from_mineralized_waters)). The WHO gives an average taste threshold for sodium in water of about 200 mg/L ([WHO](https://cdn.who.int/media/docs/default-source/wash-documents/water-safety-and-quality/dwq-guidelines-4/gdwq4-with-add1-chap10.pdf)). The SCA target of "at or near 10 mg/L" is consensus ([SCAA 2009 copy](https://www.urbansaqua.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/www.urbansaqua.com-robust-ro-drinking-water-systems-water-standards.pdf)), and the 30 mg/L ceiling quoted in summaries of the 2018 Handbook is consensus too ([Gota](https://gota.cafe/en/books/sca-water-quality-handbook)). No study found gives dose-response data for sodium between 10 and 100 mg/L in coffee.

Softened tap water is the worst case for sodium. Ion-exchange softening swaps calcium and magnesium for sodium and leaves all the bicarbonate in place. By my arithmetic it adds about 0.46 mg/L of sodium for every mg/L of hardness (as CaCO₃) removed, so softening a 250 mg/L-hardness water adds roughly 115 mg/L of sodium. Gardner (1958), as cited by Navarini and Rivetti, found that "carbonates and bicarbonates with excessive sodium ions" slowed brewing in proportion to their concentration. He advised against sodium softening of high-bicarbonate water ([Food Chemistry](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0308814609004725)). La Marzocco warns against sodium- and hydrogen-form softeners for its machines ([LM Water Specifications](https://lamarzoccousa.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/Water-Specifications.pdf)).

**Chloride versus sulfate** has no controlled coffee study. The "chloride rounds and sweetens, sulfate dries and sharpens" rule comes from beer brewing. There, a sulfate-to-chloride ratio above 2:1 is said to "favor hop perception (dry, bitter, crisp)" ([Brewer's Friend](https://www.brewersfriend.com/2018/02/13/brewing-water-basics-putting-it-all-together)). Coffee sites, including APAX Lab's own blog, carry the rule over without data ([APAX Ion Triangle](https://apaxlab.com/blogs/news/apax-ion-triangle-the-role-of-ions-on-coffee)). The one informal coffee test that looked at it found that "the chloride and sulphate ions don't seem to have much effect on flavour at the concentrations we typically use" ([Five Senses](https://fivesenses.com.au/blogs/news/experimenting-with-the-effect-of-water-quality-on-coffee)). The SCA Handbook says only that high chloride and sulfate "can make water taste salty and cause gustatory imbalance" ([pdfcoffee copy](https://pdfcoffee.com/the-water-quality-handbook-by-sca-pdf-free.html)).

**Potassium** has no coffee study at all. APAX claims potassium bicarbonate "elevate[s] both sweetness and body" but gives no data ([APAX blog](https://apaxlab.com/blogs/news/apax-ion-triangle-the-role-of-ions-on-coffee)). Roasted coffee is itself rich in potassium ([Foods 2021](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8227654/)), so a few mg/L from the water is unlikely to matter as a cation. Any effect of KHCO₃ is almost certainly the bicarbonate's effect.

## Espresso and roast change the arithmetic more than the chemistry

The best-reasoned position on espresso is a stoichiometric argument from ZHAW's Marco Wellinger, not an experiment. In espresso, "only 10% of the alkalinity is available to buffer coffee acids compared with filter coffee". The reason is that far less water touches each gram of coffee. Filter recommendations "should be scaled up for espresso preparations according to the beverage ratio used" ([SCA 25 Magazine](https://sca.coffee/sca-news/25/issue-9/english/water-and-coffee-acidity-how-to-adapt-your-water-for-different-extraction-methods-25-magazine-issue-9-pxjby)).

My arithmetic shows the size of the effect. A 60 g/L filter brew at 40 mg/L alkalinity delivers about **0.67 mg of CaCO₃-equivalent buffer per gram of coffee** (16.7 mL/g × 40 mg/L). An espresso using about 2.5 mL of water per gram at 80 mg/L delivers about **0.2 mg/g**. So espresso at the top of La Marzocco's alkalinity range still buffers its coffee less than filter coffee does at the SCA target. This grade-C argument is the basis for putting the espresso flavour penalty on a separate scale.

Bicarbonate also has physical effects in espresso. As the brew's pH drops from about 7 to about 5, bicarbonate releases CO₂. That inflates the crema and can compact the bed. Navarini and Rivetti report that bicarbonate-rich water gave abundant initial foam with poor persistence, and that ultrapure water gave less foam than calcium/magnesium water ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/223367562_Water_quality_for_Espresso_coffee)). Gardner's old finding that ion species change flow through the bed points to brew time as a possible water lever too ([Food Chemistry](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0308814609004725)). A figure circulating online says 1000 ppm bicarbonate lengthened extraction by up to 55%. It comes from a trade magazine, not the paper itself ([BeanScene](https://www.beanscenemag.com.au/how-minerals-modulate-taste-and-their-role-in-extraction/)). No modern controlled espresso study separating calcium, magnesium and bicarbonate was found.

The roast-level evidence is unresolved:
- **Liu et al. (2025, grade A):** low-hardness water suits light roasts and higher-hardness water suits dark roasts ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0889157525008026)).
- **Kang et al. (2022, grade A, consumers):** purified water was most liked at medium and dark roasts ([Food Quality and Preference](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0950329322001069)).
- **Crown lab (grade C):** magnesium suits light roasts and becomes cloying on dark roasts. Alkalinity-only waters "performed at their best when applied to darker roast styles" ([Daily Coffee News, Part II](https://dailycoffeenews.com/2018/08/29/a-practical-water-guide-for-coffee-professionals-part-ii-the-sensory-data/)).

The specific alkalinity-by-roast numbers on retailer blogs contradict one another and cite no data. A simple acid-budget idea fits the panel observations without being tested. Dark roasts have less titratable acid, so buffering that would flatten a light roast removes only sharpness from a dark one. The site can present this as a hypothesis.

No study found tests water against processing method (washed versus natural).

### The espresso-machine safety envelope

Machine limits are manufacturer specifications. They are grade C as science, but warranties depend on them, so the site should treat them as hard constraints. The numbers below are in mg/L (ppm), with hardness and alkalinity expressed as CaCO₃.

| Parameter | La Marzocco (current) | Slayer | Synesso (S-Series manual) | SCA reference | Strict intersection |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Total hardness | 70–100 | 2–6 gpg (≈ 34–103) | 50–85 | 50–175 (2016 chart) | **70–85** |
| Alkalinity | 40–80 | 40–100 | < 100 (ES1 retailer table: 40–70) | 40–70/75 | **40–70 to 40–80** |
| Chloride | 0–30 | ≤ 30 | 5–15 (ES1 table: ≤ 28, steel boilers only) | — | **≤ 30; ≤ 15 for Synesso** |
| TDS | 90–150 | 50–200 | 30–200 | 75–250 (2009) | **90–150** |
| pH | 6.5–8 (Micra manual: 8.5) | older manuals 7–9 | 6.5–8 | 6.5–7.5 (2009) | **6.5–8** |
| Chlorine | free 0–0.05, total 0–0.1 | < 0.1 | 0 | 0 | **≤ 0.1** |
| LSI | ≈ 0.1–0.7 (LM calculator) | — | — | BH: 0.2–0.7 | **slightly positive** |

Sources: [La Marzocco UK](https://www.lamarzocco.com/uk/en/technical-questions/la-marzocco-water-specifications/), [LM USA PDF](https://lamarzoccousa.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/Water-Specifications.pdf), [Linea Micra manual](https://lamarzoccousa.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/MAN.31.05.1_MICRA_EN-FR_V1.154-min.pdf), [LM water calculator](https://techcenter.lamarzocco.com/en/watercalculator), [Slayer KB](https://slayer.zohodesk.com/portal/en/kb/articles/water-treatment-requirements), [Synesso S-Series manual](https://ep-shopify.s3.amazonaws.com/related-documents/synesso/sseries/synesso-s-series-technical-manual.pdf), [Synesso ES1 retailer table](https://procoffeegear.com/products/synesso-es1), [Barista Hustle LSI lesson](https://www.baristahustle.com/lesson/twc-3-01-the-langelier-saturation-index/), [beansandbarista SCA comparison](https://beansandbarista.com/blog/sca-water-standard/).

The Langelier Saturation Index (LSI) is calculated from pH, calcium hardness, alkalinity, TDS and temperature, and it rises with temperature. Boiler water is therefore more scale-prone than the cold-water value suggests. Hach's method notes that the LSI "is not quantitative" and should be read as a guide ([Hach method 8073](https://images.hach.com/cms-portals/hach_com/cms/documents/pdf/Methods-Guidelines/Langelier-aggressive-indices-method-8073.pdf)). Chloride pitting of 316L stainless steel depends jointly on temperature, potential and chloride concentration, so the 30 mg/L figure is a practical rule rather than a universal threshold ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/287089248_Limiting_conditions_for_pitting_corrosion_of_stainless_steel_EN_14404_316L_in_terms_of_temperature_potential_and_chloride_concentration)). Softeners and decarbonising cartridges do not remove chloride; reverse osmosis does ([Home-Barista](https://www.home-barista.com/water/concern-about-high-chloride-water-t82213.html)). None of the manufacturer specifications found sets a sulfate limit.

Chloride is what actually limits espresso recipes built from concentrates. Every 1 mg/L of hardness (as CaCO₃) supplied by CaCl₂ or MgCl₂ adds about **0.71 mg/L of chloride**. This gives three consequences (my arithmetic):
- An all-chloride recipe reaches the 30 mg/L limit at about **42 mg/L of hardness**, and the Synesso 15 mg/L limit at about **21 mg/L**.
- Water that meets La Marzocco's 70 mg/L hardness minimum cannot be built from chloride salts alone while staying within its chloride limit.
- Espresso water needs sulfate, bicarbonate or citrate as part of its hardness source, or a mineral base water low in chloride.

The site's current `espresso` profile (GH 40–90, KH 30–70, Cl ≤ 30) starts below both La Marzocco's 70 hardness floor and the SCA's 40 alkalinity floor. The SCA ties that alkalinity floor to corrosion protection at boiling temperature ([Daily Coffee News, Part II](https://dailycoffeenews.com/2018/08/29/a-practical-water-guide-for-coffee-professionals-part-ii-the-sensory-data/)).

## Concentrates sell flavour names, not disclosed chemistry

**APAX Lab** sells three core blends: TONIK [1], JAMM [2] and LYLAC [3]. A fourth, KONFLUX [K], began as a 2025 World Brewers Cup limited edition and was released generally in early 2026 ([apaxlab.com Box Set](https://apaxlab.com/products/box-set); [Kaffebox KONFLUX](https://www.kaffebox.no/product/apax-lab-konflux-mineral-concentrate/)). Two things stand out when these claims are set against the evidence. First, KONFLUX claims more sweetness with **no magnesium at all**, which undercuts the lore that magnesium is the sweetness mineral. Second, LYLAC is the only product containing sulfate and is the one marketed for florals. Model v1 does the opposite: it gates florals *down* as sulfate rises. Neither claim has data behind it. The most plausible lever behind "JAMM rounds off sharp acids" would be a larger bicarbonate share, but APAX does not disclose its bicarbonate content, so the site cannot check this. No independent lab analysis or blind tasting of any APAX product was found. KONFLUX's reported SCA Best New Product 2026 award comes from retailers and is a product award, not a sensory validation ([Kaffebox](https://www.kaffebox.no/product/apax-lab-konflux-mineral-concentrate/)). Most public endorsements come from stockists, Scott Rao among them.

APAX's dose arithmetic is expressed only as TDS-meter readings:
- The standard dose is **3–4 g of concentrate per litre**, which "perform[s] best within the range of 90–120 ppm" ([APAX Recipe Guide](https://apaxlab.com/blogs/news/apax-lab-recipes)).
- **1 drop ≈ 0.065 g ≈ 2 ppm in 1 L** ([APAX Dilution Chart](https://apaxlab.com/blogs/news/apax-lab-dilution-chart)).
- The FAQ's example (2 g/L adds 60 ppm) implies **≈ 30 ppm per g/L** ([APAX FAQ](https://apaxlab.com/pages/faq)).
- Blends are written in g/L as TONIK/JAMM/LYLAC; for example, "2/1/1" means 4 g/L in total, or about 120 ppm ([APAX how-to](https://apaxlab.com/blogs/news/crafting-your-own-water-for-coffee-using-mineral-concentrates)).

The meter's conversion factor (NaCl or "442") is not stated, so these readings are not mg/L of salts and cannot be converted to GH or KH.

APAX says that in an espresso machine "you can use a small amount… (1 g/L max)". Above that, "certain minerals / ions can damage the boiler… in the long run" ([APAX FAQ](https://apaxlab.com/pages/faq)). One retailer tells customers never to put APAX in the machine, and to add 2–3 drops to the finished espresso instead ([Nordic Brew Lab](https://nordicbrewlab.com/pages/apax-lab)).

My inference is that the 1 g/L cap probably comes from chloride. If about 30 ppm of meter reading corresponds roughly to 30 mg/L of salts, then even if all of it were MgCl₂ the chloride would stay near 22 mg/L. At 3–4 g/L, the same worst case reaches roughly 67–89 mg/L. The other side of the cap is that 1 g/L in demineralised water gives only about 30 ppm. That is far below La Marzocco's 90 TDS and 70 hardness minimums, so APAX's own "machine-safe" dose produces water that is aggressive (corrosive) rather than scale-forming. The site should flag both risks.

APAX is "designed for distilled or demineralised water". If a softer base is used, it should ideally be under 30 ppm, and the dose is reduced by the base's ppm ([APAX FAQ](https://apaxlab.com/pages/faq)). Volvic, with a TDS of about 109 mg/L ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volvic_(mineral_water))), already sits near APAX's whole target range before any concentrate is added.

**Lotus Water Drops** come as four single-salt bottles: CaCl₂, MgCl₂, NaHCO₃ and KHCO₃ ([Lotus](https://lotuscoffeeproducts.com/products/lotus-water-1)). For per-country recommendations, the arithmetic is linear: final ion = (base volume × base ion + Σ dose × per-unit increment) ÷ final volume. That works exactly for Lotus and approximately for Aquacode and TWW. For APAX it works only as TDS-meter ppm. One more approximation applies: bottled-water labels report dry residue at 180 °C, which is not the same quantity as a conductivity-meter ppm reading.

Two non-model files carry the same lore and should be updated together with the model. `docs/ARCHITECTURE.md` describes GH as "how well the water extracts flavour" and says that "Mg is often said to emphasise fruit/acidity, Ca body". The `espresso` profile sits below manufacturer minimums, as noted above.

**What the site can state with confidence:**
- Bicarbonate/alkalinity is the strongest water lever. Higher values mute acidity, fruit and complexity and let roast and bitterness dominate.
- Water pH adds little once alkalinity is known.
- Calcium and magnesium at normal levels have small or undetectable flavour effects in controlled tests.
- Concentrates can add minerals but cannot remove bicarbonate; only dilution lowers it.
- Softened tap water combines high sodium with unchanged bicarbonate.
- The machine limits in the envelope table apply, as does the chloride arithmetic for chloride-based concentrates.
- APAX ingredient lists are as declared, and APAX discloses no quantities.
- Lotus per-drop increments follow the table above.

**What must be labelled as uncertain:**
- Magnesium-versus-calcium flavour roles, where panels contradict each other.
- Any roast-specific water recommendation.
- Whether very soft or distilled water tastes thin and sour, or clean.
- The sodium threshold.
- The ten-fold espresso scaling (expert reasoning, grade C).
- TDS-to-body links.
- Consumer preference for low-TDS water. It is grade A but confounded with alkalinity, and it shows that experts and consumers may want different things.

**What should not be claimed:**
- That magnesium "increases extraction". Do not cite Hendon 2014 as flavour evidence.
- That chloride sweetens or sulfate dries coffee.
- That potassium adds sweetness or body.
- That hardness creates chocolate notes.
- That SCA targets are sensory-validated optima.
- Any APAX ion profile, GH or KH.
- That a concentrate "highlights florals" or other named flavours as a prediction. These should appear only as quoted manufacturer claims.

## Conclusion

The research turns the site's working assumption around. The common "mineral personality" model, in which magnesium is fruity and floral, calcium is round, sulfate is dry and potassium is sweet, is the least supported part of coffee-water science. A single, plain variable, the buffer that bicarbonate delivers per gram of coffee, carries almost all of the evidence. A more honest flavour model would be simpler. It would have one well-supported axis: alkalinity, scaled by brew ratio, moving the cup from bright and fruity to flat and roast-led. It would have a small, clearly labelled nudge from total minerals, and a separate panel of "what practitioners report" that is never added into the score. The machine envelope would sit alongside as a hard constraint. For concentrates, this means predicting flavour from what a dose does to KH and chloride, and quoting brand flavour names as marketing. For APAX, KH cannot be predicted until someone titrates or ICP-tests the bottles.

Two open questions are worth tracking because they could change the recommendations. The first is whether experts and ordinary drinkers want different waters. Consumer panels consistently prefer the least-mineralised water, which challenges the assumption that "some hardness is needed". The second is how roast level interacts with water, where two grade-A studies point in opposite directions. Until one study crosses graded alkalinity with roast level using a trained panel, the site's most defensible per-country advice is about bicarbonate: find the lowest-bicarbonate water available, dilute where needed, and add hardness mainly to meet machine needs rather than flavour claims.
