## 1. Hendon, Colonna-Dashwood & Colonna-Dashwood (2014): what it did, what it claimed, and how it has held up

### Takeaway
Hendon et al. (2014) was a short computational study. It used density functional theory (DFT) to calculate how strongly Na⁺, Mg²⁺ and Ca²⁺ bind to seven coffee compounds. No coffee was brewed, analysed or tasted. From those binding energies it inferred that binding strength ranks Mg > Ca > Na and that bicarbonate is detrimental. The only direct experimental test of the mechanism, Bratthäll et al. (2024) on organic acids, found that acid extraction is largely independent of Ca/Mg at drinking-water levels. That result contradicts the "magnesium extracts more" mechanism.

### Cited Findings
- **Citation and type (B).** Hendon CH, Colonna-Dashwood L, Colonna-Dashwood M. "The Role of Dissolved Cations in Coffee Extraction." *J. Agric. Food Chem.* 62(21):4947–4950 (2014), DOI 10.1021/jf501687c. It is a density functional theory study, not an experiment. — [Univ. of Bath research portal](https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/the-role-of-dissolved-cations-in-coffee-extraction/); [Bath PDF](https://purehost.bath.ac.uk/ws/files/59229026/jafc_2014.pdf)
- **Compounds modelled.** Five acids, caffeine and eugenol. Bratthäll et al. describe the work as investigating "the thermodynamic binding energies of selected organic acids, caffeine and eugenol to sodium, magnesium, and calcium ions". They report that it found dissolved cations interact with the nucleophilic motifs of solvated coffee constituents. The task brief lists the acids as lactic, malic, citric, quinic and chlorogenic. — [Bratthäll et al. 2024, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2405844024026562)
- **Claimed ranking.** Mg²⁺ gave the highest relative binding energy across compounds. Ca²⁺ followed similar trends with lower binding energies. Na⁺ bound about as strongly as water did, so its effect on extraction of the acids was "not dramatic". — [Bath PDF (search snippet)](https://purehost.bath.ac.uk/ws/files/59229026/jafc_2014.pdf)
- **Inference made by the authors.** Magnesium-rich water would bind somewhat more of the desirable flavour compounds than calcium-rich water. They noted the strong binding of undesirable acids as a counterpoint. — [Bath PDF (search snippet)](https://purehost.bath.ac.uk/ws/files/59229026/jafc_2014.pdf)
- **Press release framing (D/C, author quote).** Hendon was quoted as saying "high bicarbonate levels are bad, high magnesium ion levels increase the extraction of coffee into water and improve the taste", and that sodium-rich (softened) water "didn't help the taste". The study itself measured no taste. — [Phys.org, June 2014](https://phys.org/news/2014-06-science-coffee.html)
- **Secondary over-reach (D).** Media relayed that "magnesium in hard water … sticks to eugenol, giving the resulting brew an even woodsier taste". Other sites claim Mg gives "brighter, more vibrant extraction … enhanced sweetness and clarity". Neither claim is a measured result. — [Bath Echo](https://www.bathecho.co.uk/news/bath-uni-researches-science-behind-perfect-coffee-57087/); [Pure Kopi Luwak blog](https://www.purekopiluwak.com/best-water-for-coffee/)
- **Even secondary readers note the limit of the model.** Coffee Ad Astra summarises that "the model doesn't predict big differences in the flavour of the brew, so much as in the overall extraction". — [Coffee Ad Astra (Gagné), 2018](https://coffeeadastra.com/2018/12/16/water-for-coffee-extraction/)
- **Follow-on book (C).** Colonna-Dashwood & Hendon, *Water for Coffee* (2015), argued that magnesium is "a little more efficient", that they prefer its flavour, and that it avoids limescale. Later commentary says this led to "an excessive focus on magnesium". — [Coffee Chronicler](https://coffeechronicler.com/best-water-for-coffee/); [Daily Coffee News Part I](https://dailycoffeenews.com/2018/08/15/a-practical-water-guide-for-coffee-professionals-part-i/)
- **Direct experimental challenge (A).** Bratthäll, Figueira & Nording (Heliyon 2024) state that their findings "contradict previously suggested mechanisms for the role of calcium and magnesium in extraction of acids". They found acid extraction "proceeds independent of the water composition" in most cases. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2405844024026562); [PubMed 38434259](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38434259/)
- **Thesis precursor (A-, undergraduate, not peer reviewed but chemically measured).** Bratthäll's 2021 Umeå bachelor thesis concluded that its results "support the theory that dissolved cations affect perception rather than extraction of flavorsome compounds in coffee". — [DiVA diva2:1546046](https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:1546046/FULLTEXT01.pdf)
- **Sensory challenge (A).** The University of Copenhagen 2026 study found that hardness alone produced "no marked flavor changes", even with calcium above drinking-water levels. Alkalinity was the main driver of sensory change (see Section 2). The paper was published 12 years after Hendon. — [KU research portal](https://researchprofiles.ku.dk/en/publications/impact-of-water-composition-on-coffee-sensory-properties-a-compre/)

### Inferences
- **Why binding energy is not extraction.** Hendon's binding energies say how strongly an isolated cation would bind a ligand. They do not show that a cation pulls more of a compound out of the coffee particle. The cations are present at around 0.5–2 mM, while the coffee solutes reach tens of mM in the brew. The coffee bed also brings its own K⁺, Mg²⁺ and Ca²⁺ in far larger amounts. So a thermodynamic "binding" story need not translate into higher yield. The Bratthäll null result is consistent with this.
- **What should be cited from it.** The defensible reading of Hendon 2014 is a hypothesis-generating computational paper (grade B). It should not be cited as evidence that magnesium "brings out fruit" or "increases extraction". The flavour claims came from press framing and the later book, not from data.
- **Mg vs Ca at equal hardness.** Hendon's Mg > Ca ranking is the origin of most "magnesium = brightness/fruit" marketing (see Section 3).

### Gaps
- I could not open the paper (ACS and the Bath PDF were unreachable), so I could not record its exact binding-energy values, functional, basis set or solvation treatment. I also could not confirm whether the five acids were exactly lactic, malic, citric, quinic and chlorogenic. Verify against the PDF.
- I found no formal published comment or rebuttal to Hendon 2014. The critiques are implicit, in later experimental papers and in practitioner commentary.

## 2. Experimental studies on cations, hardness, extraction and flavour

### Takeaway
Controlled experiments that separate hardness from alkalinity show three things:
- Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺ at realistic levels barely change organic-acid extraction (Bratthäll 2024).
- Calcium hardness alone barely changes sensory profile (Copenhagen 2026).
- Bicarbonate/alkalinity is the dominant driver: it lowers acidity, fruit and complexity and raises roasted/bitter notes (Copenhagen 2026).

Studies using natural or bottled waters (Korean, Turkish, Japanese, DergiPark) do find water effects, but hardness is confounded with alkalinity, sodium and total dissolved solids (TDS). Several of them find consumers prefer low-mineral or soft water.

### Cited Findings

#### Bratthäll, Figueira & Nording 2024, *Heliyon* 10(5):e26625 (Umeå University) — grade A
- **Design.** MgCl₂ and CaCl₂ were added pre- and post-brew. Citric, malic, lactic and quinic acids were measured by GC-MS and NMR. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38434259/); [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/32b12f7ec2a54c689511ea241d4a4a37)
- **Concentrations.** Post-brew samples received final concentrations of 100 ppm MgCl₂, 1000 ppm MgCl₂, 100 ppm CaCl₂ and 1000 ppm CaCl₂ (from search snippet). — [Cell/Heliyon full text](https://www.cell.com/heliyon/fulltext/S2405-8440(24)02656-2); [DiVA publisher PDF](http://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:1852083/FULLTEXT01.pdf)
- **Result at drinking-water levels.** "At concentrations typically found in drinking water, the salts resulted in limited variation of the acid content, while ten-fold higher salt concentrations produced more pronounced variations." — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2405844024026562)
- **Pre- vs post-brew.** There was no significant difference in citric acid between pre- and post-brew addition of 1000 ppm CaCl₂. For malic, lactic and quinic acid, pre- vs post-brew comparisons were generally not significant. The exceptions were lactic acid with 1000 ppm MgCl₂ and quinic acid with 100 and 1000 ppm MgCl₂ (the direction and size of these effects were truncated in the snippet). — [Cell/Heliyon](https://www.cell.com/heliyon/fulltext/S2405-8440(24)02656-2)
- **Conclusion and caveat.** Extraction "proceeds independent of the water composition" in most cases, although "interactions taking place post-brew may, however, influence the perceived flavor". — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38434259/)
- **Perception thresholds quoted in the paper.** "The concentrations at which minerals become perceivable in coffee (300 ppm Ca²⁺, 200 ppm Mg²⁺) are, however, typically not reached in tap water." — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2405844024026562)
- **Authors' framing.** Water quality recommendations for the coffee industry "still lack a scientific basis". — [DOAJ abstract](https://doaj.org/article/32b12f7ec2a54c689511ea241d4a4a37)

#### Bratthäll 2021 bachelor thesis, Umeå (diva2:1546046) — grade A- (chemical measurement, not peer reviewed)
- **Design.** MgCl₂ and CaCl₂ were added at two concentrations, pre- and post-extraction, with n = 5 per group. Lactic, malic and citric acids were semi-quantified by GC-MS. — [DiVA](https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:1546046/FULLTEXT01.pdf)
- **Results.** At the lower concentration there was no significant effect on any acid. At the high concentration, cations added pre- and post-brew significantly reduced malic and citric acid. Because the reduction occurred post-brew too, it is a solution interaction (for example complexation or precipitation), not an extraction effect. The thesis concludes that cations "affect perception rather than extraction". — [DiVA](https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:1546046/FULLTEXT01.pdf)
- **Contradiction note.** A Medium summary says the thesis found "no effect". That overstates it, since there were significant effects at high concentration. — [Medium, coffee-umami](https://medium.com/@coffee-umami/en-divalent-cations-pre-brew-or-post-brew-76e26e23da2e)

#### Rune et al. 2026, *J. Food Sci.* 91(9):e71434, DOI 10.1111/1750-3841.71434 (University of Copenhagen) — grade A
- **Design.** Brews made with ultrapure water (UPW) as the reference were compared with UPW plus Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺ and HCO₃⁻. Levels ran from the low end of the "ideal brewing zone" up to experimental extremes. The coffee was a Colombian Kachalu, and screening used duo-trio tests (from the listing). — [KU portal](https://researchprofiles.ku.dk/en/publications/impact-of-water-composition-on-coffee-sensory-properties-a-compre/); [QxMD listing](https://read.qxmd.com/read/42746805/impact-of-water-composition-on-coffee-sensory-properties-a-comprehensive-analysis)
- **Hardness result.** "Water hardness alone produced no marked flavor changes, even when calcium was added at concentrations above those typically found in drinking water." — [KU portal](https://researchprofiles.ku.dk/en/publications/impact-of-water-composition-on-coffee-sensory-properties-a-compre/)
- **Ca + bicarbonate result.** The small differences in Ca + HCO₃⁻ samples were attributed to "reductions in effective bicarbonate concentration due to CaCO₃ precipitation rather than to any direct flavor contribution from calcium". — [KU portal](https://researchprofiles.ku.dk/en/publications/impact-of-water-composition-on-coffee-sensory-properties-a-compre/)
- **Bicarbonate result.** Increasing bicarbonate (up to 300 mg/L at the extreme) "substantially reduced perceived acidity, fruitiness, and complexity, while increasing roasted and bitter notes". — [KU portal](https://researchprofiles.ku.dk/en/publications/impact-of-water-composition-on-coffee-sensory-properties-a-compre/)
- **Conclusion.** "Alkalinity, not hardness, was the primary determinant of sensory changes … calcium variations in realistic ranges showed little sensory relevance." — [KU portal](https://researchprofiles.ku.dk/en/publications/impact-of-water-composition-on-coffee-sensory-properties-a-compre/)

#### Navarini & Rivetti 2010, *Food Chemistry* 122(2):424–428, DOI 10.1016/j.foodchem.2009.04.019 (illycaffè) — grade B (review with some data)
- **Scope.** A review of water/coffee interactions for espresso that discusses "the role played by alkalinity and selected cations on sensory properties of Espresso coffee". It notes little awareness of the direct effect of water composition on espresso quality. — [FAO AGRIS record](https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/65de275f4c5aef494fda723f); [ScienceDirect abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0308814609004725)
- **Crema/foam (secondary).** Espresso made with Milli-Q ultrapure water had lower foam volume than espresso made with Ca/Mg-containing water, and "foam volume depended on the bicarbonate ion content". This comes from a secondary summary I could not check against the paper. — [Eau Noire blog summary](https://eaunoirecoffee.com/best-water-for-espresso/); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/223367562_Water_quality_for_Espresso_coffee)
- **Softened water and brew time (secondary).** Blog summaries report that the paper tested ultrapure, very hard tap and softened tap water, and that softening can greatly extend espresso brew time. Not verified. — [AGRIS](https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/65de275f4c5aef494fda723f)

#### Gardner 1958 (via Navarini & Rivetti 2010) — grade B (historic, second-hand)
- **Flow.** "The concentration and species of ions in solution change the rate at which water passes through the bed of ground coffee, thereby causing differential extraction of coffee solids." — [ScienceDirect, Navarini & Rivetti](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0308814609004725)
- **Brew time.** "Carbonates and bicarbonates with excessive sodium ions" retarded brewing time in direct relation to their concentration. All other ions normally present in municipal water "appeared to have little effect, if any". Gardner advised against ion-exchange softening (Ca/Mg replaced by Na) for high-bicarbonate water. — [ScienceDirect, Navarini & Rivetti](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0308814609004725)

#### Lockhart, Tucker & Merritt 1955, *J. Food Sci.* 20:598–605, "The effect of water impurities on the flavor of brewed coffee" (Coffee Brewing Institute) — grade A (historic)
- **Citation only.** The citation is confirmed, but I could not see the findings. — [Wiley](https://ift.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1365-2621.1955.tb16874.x); [SCA News on the Coffee Brewing Institute](https://scanews.coffee/2013/10/04/the-coffee-brewing-institute/)

#### Pangborn, Trabue & Little 1971, *J. Food Sci.* 36(2):355–362 — grade A (historic, trained panel)
- **Design.** Coffee, tea and flavoured drinks were made from solutions of eight minerals at 750 ppm, and from six natural waters with 42–1,725 ppm TDS. — [Wiley abstract](https://ift.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1365-2621.1971.tb04061.x)
- **Main results.** Carbonate solutions gave the least desirable beverages ("flat and insipid"). Coffee made with distilled water was "excessively sour". — [Wiley abstract](https://ift.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1365-2621.1971.tb04061.x)
- **Per-salt results (secondary, unverified).** At 750 ppm, NaHCO₃ gave the strongest bitterness and CaCl₂ the strongest sourness. NaCl made coffee slightly salty. CaCl₂ and MgCl₂ showed no significant difference from each other. — [Search summary citing the paper](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/229783358_Analysis_of_coffee_tea_and_artificially_flavored_drinks_prepared_from_mineralized_waters)

#### Kyungpook National University 2020, *Korean J. Food Sci. Technol.* — grade A (consumer panel)
- **Design.** Coffee was brewed with distilled (L), medium-hardness (M) and high-hardness (H) water. Organic acids, caffeine, chlorogenic acid and eugenol were measured, plus a consumer test with 103 people (56 women, 47 men, university students). — [KNU portal](https://research.knu.ac.kr/en/publications/effect-of-water-hardness-on-coffee-composition-and-coffee-prefere/); [KoreaScience PDF](https://www.koreascience.kr/article/JAKO202031458603203.pdf)
- **Chemistry.** Organic acids were highest with high-hardness water. Chlorogenic acid and caffeine were highest with distilled water. — [KNU portal](https://research.knu.ac.kr/en/publications/effect-of-water-hardness-on-coffee-composition-and-coffee-prefere/)
- **Sensory.** Distilled-water coffee was most preferred (≥42%). Bitterness, acidity, body and balance did not differ significantly among samples. The authors suggest students' preferences differ from those of experts. — [KNU portal](https://research.knu.ac.kr/en/publications/effect-of-water-hardness-on-coffee-composition-and-coffee-prefere/)

#### Korean commercial-water QDA study 2017, *Culinary Sci. & Hospitality Res.* 23(4), DOI 10.20878/cshr.2017.23.4.011 — grade A (trained QDA panel plus hedonic test)
- **Design and results.** Five commercial waters plus distilled water were compared. Flavour, acidity, bitterness, sweetness, body and aftertaste all differed by water (p < 0.01). Canonical correlation linked magnesium most strongly to bitterness, and bitterness was inversely related to acidity and sweetness. Liking fell as total mineral content rose. — [KoreaScience](https://koreascience.kr/article/JAKO201721242143947.page)

#### Kang, Piao & Ko 2022, *Food Quality and Preference* 101:104631 — grade A (consumer study)
- **Design.** Purified (~0 ppm TDS), tap (~100 ppm) and Australian bottled (~200 ppm) water were tested across roast points. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0950329322001069)
- **Results.** Liking decreased with increasing TDS at medium and dark roasts, where purified water was preferred. Most attributes differed significantly, except aftertaste. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0950329322001069)

#### Dadalı 2021, *Gıda* (Ege University), Turkish coffee — grade A
- **Results.** With soft, medium-hard and hard water, bitterness was perceived most intensely with hard water. Roasted coffee and roasted hazelnut notes were strongest with medium-hard water, which also gave the most volatiles (30 compounds). Sweetness and sourness were highest with soft water. — [DergiPark](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/pub/gida/article/938427); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/354390949_INFLUENCE_OF_WATER_HARDNESS_ON_VOLATILE_COMPOUNDS_AND_SENSORY_PROPERTIES_OF_TURKISH_COFFEE)
- **Companion filter-coffee study.** Hardness affected the flavour of filter coffee, and the author recommends medium-hard water for more volatiles. — [TURJAF](https://agrifoodscience.com/index.php/TURJAF/article/view/3885)

#### Turkish bottled-water study (DergiPark, article-file 4984487) — grade A (abstract only)
- **Design.** Five bottled waters (A–E) were used in a Clever Dripper, with extraction yield (EY), TDS, pH and a sensory panel of 30 semi-trained panellists. — [DergiPark PDF](https://dergipark.org.tr/tr/download/article-file/4984487)
- **Results.** The highest extraction yield came from the water high in Ca and bicarbonate, but it did not score highest in sensory tests. Waters A and D, with "more balanced mineral profiles", had the best acceptability. Ca/Mg "appeared to help body and acidity", while high Na/Cl reduced aroma and balance. The authors conclude that quality depends on the ionic composition, not just the amount extracted. — [DergiPark PDF](https://dergipark.org.tr/tr/download/article-file/4984487)

#### Liu, Lin, Hu, … Yi, Li 2025 (ScienceDirect S0889157525008026; likely *J. Food Compos. Anal.*; SSRN 5181791) — grade A
- **Roast-dependent hardness effect.** "Water samples of high/low hardness values benefited the extraction of taste compounds and the exhibition of secondary dominant tastes for coffees of dark/light roasting degrees, respectively." — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0889157525008026); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5181791)
- **Proposed mechanisms.** Water penetration into particles (from water-retention analysis) and water's ability to release taste compounds from coffee proteins (from fluorescence, circular dichroism and molecular dynamics). Cations influence the second mechanism. Slower drawdown also improved extraction. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0889157525008026)

#### Imamura 2024, *Food Sci. Technol. Res.* 30(2) — grade A- (small panel plus taste sensor)
- **Result.** Water from melted ice, which is low in sodium, gave coffee a cleaner taste. Adding sodium back raised taste-sensor saltiness and reduced "clean taste". — [J-STAGE](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/fstr/30/2/30_FSTR-D-23-00129/_html/-char/en); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/377709196_The_clean_taste_of_coffee_depends_on_the_sodium_content_of_its_extraction_water)

#### Fibrianto et al. 2018, IOP Conf. Ser. Earth Environ. Sci. 102:012095 — grade A- (conference paper)
- **Result.** Brewing water affected the intensity of sweet and chocolate aroma and oily mouthfeel. Taste and flavour were not affected by pH in the range 5.5–9.1. — [IOPscience](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1755-1315/102/1/012095)

#### ZHAW Coffee Excellence Center (Wellinger, Smrke, Yeretzian) — grade B/C
- **Book chapter.** "Water for Extraction — Composition, Recommendations, and Treatment", in *The Craft and Science of Coffee* (2017). It frames water by total hardness, alkalinity and pH, and co-developed the SCAE Water Chart. — [ZHAW digital collection](https://digitalcollection.zhaw.ch/entities/publication/65a2bd50-fa68-4832-9b7b-ef56f073a259); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/312183310_Water_for_Extraction-Composition_Recommendations_and_Treatment); [SCAE Water Chart report](https://brandconnect.gr/wp-content/uploads/2021/01/SCAE-water-chart-report.pdf)
- **Mg/Ca-ratio cupping (C, vendor-reported).** Total hardness was fixed at 70 ppm CaCO₃ and alkalinity at 40 ppm CaCO₃ while the Mg:Ca ratio varied. The blind triplicate used three qualified cuppers. BWT reports that "shifting the mineral ratio … towards more magnesium decreased bitterness and astringency significantly". BWT sells magnesium cartridges (conflict of interest). — [BWT Magnesium Technology](https://www.bwt-wam.com/en/technologies/bwt-magnesium-technology/); [Café Europa reprint, BWT](https://www.bwt.com/en/-/media/bwt/global-data/documents/literatur-pou-ps/reprint_cafe-eu_winter_2016_22-26.pdf)
- **2015 cupping (C).** "Alkalinity significantly affects the sensory attributes of a coffee". Low total hardness gave a "sweeter taste" (secondary summary). — [ResearchGate chapter](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/312183310_Water_for_Extraction-Composition_Recommendations_and_Treatment)

#### SCA partner article "Water and the Taste of Coffee" — grade C
- **Claims.** Overall mineralisation and alkalinity (carbonate hardness) matter most. Na, Mg and Ca "play a more minor role". A "theoretically better extraction through a greater proportion of magnesium … went unrecognized by 14 testers in a blind taste test". — [SCA News](https://sca.coffee/sca-news/news-from-our-partners/water-and-the-taste-of-coffee-ay9gh)

### Inferences
- **Confounding in natural-water studies.** In the natural- and bottled-water studies (Korean 2017 and 2020, Turkish, Kang 2022, Pangborn), "hardness" travels together with bicarbonate, sodium and TDS. Their hardness effects, such as bitterness with hard water and preference for soft water, are probably driven partly or mainly by alkalinity. The Copenhagen design, which adds Ca/Mg to UPW without bicarbonate, is the cleanest test, and it found almost nothing from hardness alone.
- **Two different questions.** "Extraction" (EY, TDS, acids) and "flavour" have to be modelled separately. The only chemistry data at realistic concentrations (Bratthäll) show little extraction change. The flavour effects that are robust come from bicarbonate.
- **Contradictions to record:**
  - Hendon (Mg > Ca increases extraction) versus Bratthäll (acid extraction is independent of Ca/Mg).
  - BWT/ZHAW (more Mg lowers bitterness and astringency) versus the SCA partner article (14 blind testers could not detect Mg-rich water) and the Korean 2017 study (Mg correlated with bitterness).
  - Turkish DergiPark (Ca/Mg help body and acidity) versus Copenhagen (calcium has little sensory relevance).
  - Distilled water: Pangborn 1971 found it "excessively sour", yet consumers in Kang 2022 and KNU 2020 preferred purified or distilled water.

### Gaps
- The ZHAW Mg:Ca study has no peer-reviewed primary publication that I could find. The only accounts come from BWT, the sponsor.
- I could not obtain the Copenhagen study's exact concentrations, panel size or Mg-specific results. The abstract emphasises Ca and HCO₃⁻.
- Navarini & Rivetti's experimental values, and Lockhart 1955's findings, could not be verified.
- I found no peer-reviewed study measuring EY or TDS against controlled Ca/Mg at fixed alkalinity. The DergiPark study has EY, but with bottled waters.
- Volatile-compound data against controlled Ca/Mg are absent. The Turkish volatiles work used natural waters of differing hardness.
- The Seven Miles/UNSW espresso study is cited only by a water-filter vendor. I could not find a publication. — [Viomi blog](https://water.viomi.com/blogs/hydration-lab/water-quality-for-coffee-brewing-guide)

## 3. Does magnesium specifically emphasise fruit, acidity, florals or sweetness? Does calcium add body? Extraction or perception?

### Takeaway
I found no peer-reviewed controlled experiment showing that Mg²⁺ specifically enhances fruit, acidity, florals or sweetness, or that Ca²⁺ specifically adds body or creaminess. Those attributions trace back to Hendon's computational ranking, the *Water for Coffee* book, practitioner cuppings and vendor marketing (grades B–D). The best current evidence (Bratthäll 2024; Copenhagen 2026) suggests:
- at realistic levels, Ca/Mg change neither acid extraction nor the sensory profile much;
- whatever effect exists is more plausibly a post-brew perception or solution effect than an extraction effect;
- bicarbonate is the variable that moves acidity and fruit.

### Cited Findings
- **Copenhagen (A).** "Water hardness alone produced no marked flavor changes" even with Ca above drinking-water levels. Calcium variations "in realistic ranges showed little sensory relevance". — [KU portal](https://researchprofiles.ku.dk/en/publications/impact-of-water-composition-on-coffee-sensory-properties-a-compre/)
- **Bratthäll 2024 (A).** Acid extraction was independent of Ca/Mg. Post-brew interactions "may … influence the perceived flavor". — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38434259/)
- **Bratthäll 2021 thesis (A-).** Results support "perception rather than extraction". — [DiVA](https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:1546046/FULLTEXT01.pdf)
- **Korean QDA 2017 (A).** Magnesium content correlated most strongly with bitterness, which ran inversely to acidity and sweetness. This is the opposite of the "Mg = brightness" lore (correlational, natural waters). — [KoreaScience](https://koreascience.kr/article/JAKO201721242143947.page)
- **Daily Coffee News practitioner panel (C).** Magnesium gave "appealing sweet and floral notes, mostly in lighter roasts", but darker roasts drew complaints of "heavy mouthfeel and cloying sweetness". The authors call Mg an "accent" mineral. — [Daily Coffee News Part II, 2018](https://dailycoffeenews.com/2018/08/29/a-practical-water-guide-for-coffee-professionals-part-ii-the-sensory-data/)
- **BWT/ZHAW (C, vendor).** More Mg at fixed 70 ppm hardness lowered bitterness and astringency. — [BWT](https://www.bwt-wam.com/en/technologies/bwt-magnesium-technology/)
- **SCA partner article (C).** A blind test with 14 testers did not recognise Mg-rich water. — [SCA News](https://sca.coffee/sca-news/news-from-our-partners/water-and-the-taste-of-coffee-ay9gh)
- **Marketing and lore (D).** "Calcium adding body and roundness and magnesium lifting fruitiness and clarity". — [The Basic Barista](https://thebasicbarista.com/en-us/blogs/article/tds-extraction-and-balance-guide-to-water-brewing-minerals). Another site gives an unsourced 2:1 Ca:Mg ratio "from a Coffee Science Foundation" and claims "calcium drives 60–70% of extraction efficiency". — [Complete Home Barista](https://completehomebarista.com/guides/coffee-water-chemistry-espresso/)
- **Turkish DergiPark (A, abstract).** Ca and Mg "appeared to help body and acidity". The waters differed in many ions, so this is not Ca-specific. — [DergiPark PDF](https://dergipark.org.tr/tr/download/article-file/4984487)

### Inferences
- **For the prediction model.** A science-based site should not encode "Mg → fruit/florals" or "Ca → body" as established effects. Encoding them as "practitioner hypothesis, weak or contradictory evidence" would be defensible.
- **Robust levers.** The robust lever is alkalinity/bicarbonate. Higher values give less acidity, fruit and complexity and more roast and bitterness (A). Total mineral load/TDS is secondary: higher TDS lowered consumer liking in several studies (A, but confounded).
- **Plausible perception mechanism.** Divalent cations can complex with acids post-brew. Bratthäll saw reduced malic and citric acid at ten times drinking-water levels, both pre- and post-brew. This could slightly blunt perceived sourness at high concentrations. That would be a "buffering or complexation" perception effect, not an extraction effect.

### Gaps
- No study I found isolates Mg vs Ca at equal molar hardness, at zero or fixed alkalinity, with a trained descriptive panel and published statistics.
- No body or mouthfeel instrument data (viscosity, tribology) against Ca/Mg were found.

## 4. At what concentrations do effects become measurable or perceptible, compared with typical waters?

### Takeaway
- **Extraction (acids).** Changes were limited at drinking-water-like levels (100 ppm salt, about 25 mg/L Mg²⁺ or 36 mg/L Ca²⁺ if anhydrous salt was used). They became pronounced only at ten times that level.
- **Taste.** Thresholds for Ca²⁺ (about 100–300 mg/L in water, about 300 mg/L in coffee) and Mg²⁺ (cited as about 200 mg/L in coffee; very uncertain in water) are well above SCA targets and most tap or bottled waters.
- **Bicarbonate.** Effects are perceptible across realistic ranges, up to 300 mg/L tested.

### Cited Findings
- **Bratthäll 2024.** 100 ppm MgCl₂/CaCl₂ gave limited variation, while 1000 ppm gave more pronounced variation. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2405844024026562); [Cell](https://www.cell.com/heliyon/fulltext/S2405-8440(24)02656-2)
- **Thresholds in coffee as cited by Bratthäll.** "Perceivable in coffee" at 300 ppm Ca²⁺ and 200 ppm Mg²⁺, "typically not reached in tap water". — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2405844024026562)
- **Calcium in water.** The taste threshold is about 100–300 mg/L depending on the anion. — [WHO GDWQ ch.10](https://cdn.who.int/media/docs/default-source/wash-documents/water-safety-and-quality/dwq-guidelines-4/gdwq4-with-add1-chap10.pdf); [Health Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/programs/guidelines-canadian-drinking-water-quality-operational-parameters.html)
- **Magnesium in water.** WHO says the threshold is "probably lower than calcium". Health Canada cites 100–500 mg/L (bitter). A European mineral-water paper lists 45–60 mg/L. — [WHO](https://cdn.who.int/media/docs/default-source/wash-documents/water-safety-and-quality/dwq-guidelines-4/gdwq4-with-add1-chap10.pdf); [Health Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/programs/guidelines-canadian-drinking-water-quality-operational-parameters.html); [Foods 2020, PMC7765973](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7765973/). These figures disagree.
- **Calcium fortification study.** A sensory threshold test for calcium added to drinking water exists. — [PMC8753188](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8753188/)
- **Copenhagen.** Bicarbonate up to 300 mg/L was tested, with "substantial" effects. Ca "above concentrations typically found in drinking water" gave no marked change. — [KU portal](https://researchprofiles.ku.dk/en/publications/impact-of-water-composition-on-coffee-sensory-properties-a-compre/)
- **SCA targets (C).**
  - Total hardness 68 ppm CaCO₃ (acceptable 17–85).
  - Alkalinity about 40 ppm CaCO₃.
  - pH 7 (6.5–7.5).
  - TDS 150 mg/L (75–250).
  - Na about 10 mg/L.

  Sources: [Coffee Ad Astra](https://coffeeadastra.com/2018/12/16/water-for-coffee-extraction/); [Clive Coffee](https://clivecoffee.com/blogs/learn/the-science-of-water-composition-in-espresso)
- **Alkalinity floor.** 40 ppm is cited as the minimum for buffering and corrosion protection at boiling temperature. — [Daily Coffee News Part II](https://dailycoffeenews.com/2018/08/29/a-practical-water-guide-for-coffee-professionals-part-ii-the-sensory-data/)
- **Pangborn 1971.** Single salts at 750 ppm were needed to produce clear differences between some salts. Natural waters spanned 42–1,725 ppm TDS. — [Wiley](https://ift.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1365-2621.1971.tb04061.x)
- **Consumer liking vs TDS.** Liking declined from about 0 to 100 to 200 ppm TDS at medium and dark roasts. — [Kang et al. 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0950329322001069)

### Inferences
- **Unit conversion (my calculation, assumes anhydrous salts).** 100 ppm MgCl₂ ≈ 25.5 mg/L Mg²⁺ (≈105 ppm as CaCO₃). 100 ppm CaCl₂ ≈ 36 mg/L Ca²⁺ (≈90 ppm as CaCO₃). So Bratthäll's "low" dose sits at or above the SCA hardness target, and the "high" dose is roughly ten times it. If hydrated salts (MgCl₂·6H₂O, CaCl₂·2H₂O) were used, the cation doses are lower (≈12 mg/L Mg²⁺, ≈27 mg/L Ca²⁺). Check the methods section.
- **Range where effects are proven.** At SCA-zone hardness (17–85 ppm as CaCO₃, about 7–34 mg/L Ca²⁺ or 4–21 mg/L Mg²⁺), Ca/Mg concentrations are roughly an order of magnitude below the cited perception thresholds. A model should treat cation-specific flavour effects in this range as unproven. Bicarbonate effects in the 0–300 mg/L range are proven.

### Gaps
- The original source of the "300 ppm Ca / 200 ppm Mg perceivable in coffee" figures was not identified. Bratthäll cites it, but I could not see the reference.
- No dose-response curve (EY vs Ca/Mg) at fixed alkalinity was found.

## 5. Sodium and potassium

### Takeaway
- **Sodium.** The evidence is sparse but consistent. It adds saltiness and reduces "clean" taste in coffee (Imamura 2024, A-). NaCl at 750 ppm makes coffee slightly salty (Pangborn 1971, A). Sodium bicarbonate, typical of softened high-alkalinity water, is the most bitter and flat salt and retards flow (Pangborn; Gardner). The plain-water threshold is about 200 mg/L.
- **Potassium.** I found no peer-reviewed coffee study. Claims that K adds sweetness or body are vendor marketing (D).
- **Bitterness suppression.** Sodium's general bitterness-suppressing effect is not documented for coffee in the sources found.

### Cited Findings
- **Na in water.** The average taste threshold is about 200 mg/L at room temperature. — [WHO GDWQ ch.10](https://cdn.who.int/media/docs/default-source/wash-documents/water-safety-and-quality/dwq-guidelines-4/gdwq4-with-add1-chap10.pdf)
- **Imamura 2024 (A-).** Low-sodium ice-melt water gave a cleaner taste. Adding Na increased taste-sensor saltiness and reduced clean taste. The author presents the link as suggestive. — [J-STAGE](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/fstr/30/2/30_FSTR-D-23-00129/_html/-char/en)
- **Pangborn 1971 (A, secondary detail).** NaCl at 750 ppm was slightly salty. NaHCO₃ gave the strongest bitterness. Carbonate waters were "flat and insipid". — [Wiley](https://ift.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1365-2621.1971.tb04061.x)
- **Hendon 2014 (B).** Na⁺ binds about as strongly as water, so there was no notable extraction effect. Hendon said softened (Na-rich) water "didn't help the taste". — [Bath PDF](https://purehost.bath.ac.uk/ws/files/59229026/jafc_2014.pdf); [Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2014-06-science-coffee.html)
- **Gardner 1958 (via Navarini & Rivetti).** Carbonates/bicarbonates "with excessive sodium ions" retard brewing. Gardner advised against Na ion-exchange softening of high-bicarbonate water. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0308814609004725)
- **Turkish bottled-water study (A, abstract).** High Na and Cl reduced aroma and flavour balance. — [DergiPark PDF](https://dergipark.org.tr/tr/download/article-file/4984487)
- **Potassium (D).** Apax Lab claims potassium (as KHCO₃) "elevate[s] both sweetness and body". It also claims K has a lower impact threshold than Ca and that excess K adds bitterness or a metallic taste. No data are given. — [Apax Lab blog](https://apaxlab.com/blogs/news/apax-ion-triangle-the-role-of-ions-on-coffee)
- **Potassium in plain water.** Low K concentration has positive effects on mineral-water acceptance. — [Foods 2020, PMC7765973](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7765973/)

### Inferences
- **Brewed coffee already contains a lot of potassium.** Roasted coffee is rich in potassium, so brewed coffee already contains hundreds of mg/L K⁺ (see [Foods 2021 macroelements study, PMC8227654](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8227654/)). A few mg/L of K⁺ from the water is therefore unlikely to matter as a cation. Any KHCO₃ effect is most plausibly the bicarbonate (alkalinity), not the potassium.
- **Same logic for sodium bicarbonate.** NaHCO₃ effects are dominated by bicarbonate. Na itself mainly matters above roughly 50–200 mg/L (saltiness), which occurs in softened water.

### Gaps
- No coffee-specific taste threshold for K⁺ or Na⁺ was found.
- No controlled peer-reviewed test of potassium in coffee brew water was found.
- The potassium content of brewed coffee was not retrieved as a number in this session.

## 6. Espresso vs filter, and roast level

### Takeaway
- **Roast.** The roast-level interaction is the best-supported nuance. Higher-hardness (more complex) water helped dark roasts and lower-hardness water helped light roasts (Liu et al. 2025, A). Consumer preference for low-TDS water was strongest at medium and dark roasts (Kang 2022, A). Practitioner panels report Mg as pleasant in light roasts and cloying in dark (C).
- **Espresso.** Espresso evidence is mostly old or review-level. Bicarbonate and ions affect flow and brew time (Gardner 1958) and crema/foam (Navarini & Rivetti 2010). I found no modern controlled espresso study of Ca vs Mg.

### Cited Findings
- **Liu et al. 2025 (A).** High- and low-hardness water benefited extraction and "secondary dominant tastes" for dark and light roasts respectively. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0889157525008026)
- **Kang, Piao & Ko 2022 (A).** Liking decreased with water TDS at medium and dark roasts, where purified water was preferred. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0950329322001069)
- **Daily Coffee News panel (C).** Mg gave sweet and floral notes in lighter roasts, but heavy or cloying results in darker roasts. — [Daily Coffee News Part II](https://dailycoffeenews.com/2018/08/29/a-practical-water-guide-for-coffee-professionals-part-ii-the-sensory-data/)
- **Turkish coffee (boiled, unfiltered, A).** Hard water gave the most bitterness, medium-hard water the most roasted/hazelnut notes and volatiles, and soft water the most sweetness and sourness. — [DergiPark](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/pub/gida/article/938427)
- **Espresso, Gardner via Navarini (B).** Ion species and concentration change the flow rate through the bed, and bicarbonates retard brew time. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0308814609004725)
- **Espresso foam (B, secondary).** Ultrapure water gave less foam than Ca/Mg water, and foam depended on bicarbonate. — [Eau Noire summary](https://eaunoirecoffee.com/best-water-for-espresso/)
- **Bicarbonate and CO₂ (C).** High bicarbonate adds CO₂, which can impede extraction and enlarge the bloom or crema. — [Daily Coffee News Part II](https://dailycoffeenews.com/2018/08/29/a-practical-water-guide-for-coffee-professionals-part-ii-the-sensory-data/)
- **Robusta espresso.** There is a study on water quality, CO₂ degassing and sensory attributes of Lampung robusta espresso. Details were not retrieved. — [SBICafé repository](https://sbicafe.ufv.br/handle/123456789/14719)
- **No crema data (C/D survey).** No controlled data linking Ca/Mg to crema were found among industry guides. — [Search summary of Clive Coffee and others](https://clivecoffee.com/blogs/learn/the-science-of-water-composition-in-espresso)

### Inferences
- **Espresso and flavour.** Espresso uses a much higher coffee-to-water ratio. Water ions are therefore an even smaller fraction of the cup's solutes, so direct flavour effects of Ca/Mg should be smaller than in filter coffee. Bicarbonate is still neutralised by coffee acids (more of them per ml). Flow effects (Gardner) may matter more than flavour effects in espresso.
- **Roast and alkalinity.** Dark roasts have lower titratable acidity, so the same bicarbonate dose neutralises a larger share of their acids. This would help explain why "harder" (usually more alkaline) natural water tastes worse on light roasts and is tolerated better on dark. This is my mechanistic inference, consistent with Copenhagen and Liu, not directly tested.

### Gaps
- No modern peer-reviewed espresso study isolating Ca vs Mg vs HCO₃⁻ was found.
- Liu 2025's hardness values and the alkalinity of its waters were not retrieved, so it is unclear whether its "hardness" effect is really an alkalinity effect.
- No study of cold brew with controlled Ca/Mg was found. A cold-brew study with seven water types exists, but details are limited. — [Canrea Journal](https://agritech.unhas.ac.id/ojs/index.php/canrea/article/download/2240/590/7542)
