# Alkalinity/bicarbonate, pH, anions (Cl-, SO4 2-, HCO3-), sodium, and espresso-machine chemistry: evidence-graded notes

**Evidence grades used throughout:** (A) controlled experiment, peer reviewed; (B) peer reviewed but indirect (modelling, review, non-sensory chemistry, conference paper); (C) industry or professional panel, standards body, or manufacturer specification; (D) blog, forum, retailer copy or anecdote.

**Method caveat (read first):** In this session every direct page fetch failed. Publisher sites, PMC, PubMed, Europe PMC, ZHAW, SCA, BWT, Royal Coffee, Daily Coffee News, uoregon.edu (Hendon archive), pdfcoffee, diva-portal, IOP and archive.org all returned DNS or proxy-403 errors. All findings below therefore come from search-engine result extracts, which quote or paraphrase the pages linked. Where an extract quoted the source verbatim, the finding is marked "(quoted)". Where a number is reported only by a secondary page, the note says so. None of the full texts of Rune et al. 2026, Pangborn et al. 1971, Navarini & Rivetti 2010 or the ZHAW 2024 "New Target Zones" PDF could be read directly.

**Unit conversions for the report writer (my arithmetic, not from sources):**
- 1 mg/L HCO3- = 0.82 mg/L alkalinity as CaCO3.
- 1 °dKH = 17.85 mg/L as CaCO3.
- 1 US grain/gal = 17.1 mg/L as CaCO3.
- So 40 mg/L as CaCO3 ≈ 49 mg/L HCO3- ≈ 2.2 dKH. 300 mg/L HCO3- ≈ 246 mg/L as CaCO3 ≈ 13.8 dKH. 1000 mg/L HCO3- ≈ 820 mg/L as CaCO3.

## Q1. Experimental evidence that bicarbonate/alkalinity reduces acidity and fruitiness and increases flatness or bitterness (with magnitudes)

### Takeaway
The strongest and most recent controlled evidence comes from the University of Copenhagen (Rune et al., *J. Food Sci.* 2026; grade A). Adding bicarbonate to ultrapure water, up to 300 mg/L HCO3-, lowered perceived acidity, fruitiness and complexity and raised roasted and bitter notes. Calcium, magnesium and hardness within realistic ranges had little sensory effect. Older work points the same way but is weaker or uses extreme concentrations:
- Pangborn et al. 1971 used about 750 ppm mineral additions and found NaHCO3 gave the most bitter, flat coffee.
- Navarini & Rivetti 2010 is mainly about espresso foam and flow, not acidity.

I could not retrieve effect sizes (scale scores, p-values) for any of these studies. The only quantified dose point is the 300 mg/L ceiling of the Copenhagen design.

### Cited Findings

Concentrations ran "from the lower levels found within the 'ideal brewing zone' up to higher experimental extremes." The coffee and the test method are not confirmed from the primary text. — [KU portal](https://researchprofiles.ku.dk/en/publications/impact-of-water-composition-on-coffee-sensory-properties-a-compre/); [Merakitech summary (D)](https://www.merakitech.com/blogs/coffee-knowledge/espresso-water-alkalinity-the-buffer-that-changes-acidity)

- Wender Bredie's Copenhagen sensory group previously found that Danish drinking water with TDS of 300–350 mg/L was most preferred. That finding is about the taste of the water, not coffee.   - The most bitter coffee came from NaHCO3 water and the sourest from CaCl2 water.
  - "the carbonates were the worst offenders, resulting in a bitter, flat coffee."

  - — [ResearchGate/Korean review extracts via search](https://koreascience.kr/article/JAKO201721242143947.page); [BeanScene (D)](https://www.beanscenemag.com.au/how-minerals-modulate-taste-and-their-role-in-extraction/)

- **Contradiction:** secondary accounts disagree on the chloride salts. One review says high CaCl2 and MgCl2 made coffee "excessively sour." Another says "no significant difference was observed with the water containing CaCl2 or MgCl2." — [search extracts of reviews citing Pangborn](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/229783358_Analysis_of_coffee_tea_and_artificially_flavored_drinks_prepared_from_mineralized_waters)

**Navarini & Rivetti 2010 (B: review/short study in *Food Chemistry* 122(2):424–428)**
- The paper reviews how alkalinity and selected cations affect the sensory properties of espresso. — [AGRIS](https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/65de275f4c5aef494fda723f); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0308814609004725); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Water-quality-for-Espresso-coffee-Navarini-Rivetti/f5054fc09852573edcd954e51eb42edd738cdfee)

  - Espresso brewed with Milli-Q water had lower foam volume than espresso brewed with Ca/Mg-containing water.
  - Foam volume depended on bicarbonate content.
  - Bicarbonate-rich water gave "abundant initial foam" but "very poor foam persistency and… unacceptable foam texture" (quoted via forum). (B; quote is secondary) — [ResearchGate extract](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/223367562_Water_quality_for_Espresso_coffee); [Home-Barista quote](https://www.home-barista.com/water/matt-pergers-water-recipe-for-coffee-is-it-ok-safe-for-espresso-machines-what-do-you-think-t42255-50.html)

- Adding 1000 ppm bicarbonate reportedly lengthened espresso extraction time by up to 55%. This comes from a secondary account only and is unverified. (D) — [BeanScene](https://www.beanscenemag.com.au/how-minerals-modulate-taste-and-their-role-in-extraction/)

**Other studies 2015–2026**
- Liu et al. 2025, *J. Food Composition and Analysis*: a filter-brewing study crossing water hardness with roast degree. "Water samples of high/low hardness values benefited the extraction of taste compounds… for coffees of dark/light roasting degrees, respectively." (A) — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0889157525008026); [SSRN preprint](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/Delivery.cfm/f189cae0-8f86-4a1b-a8ff-a98a891a70b4-MECA.pdf?abstractid=5181791&mirid=1)
- Bratthäll, Figueira & Nording 2024, *Heliyon* (doi 10.1016/j.heliyon.2024.e26625) — [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10907646/); [Cell/Heliyon](https://www.cell.com/heliyon/fulltext/S2405-8440(24)02656-2)
  - Design: MgCl2 and CaCl2 were added before or after brewing, and citric, malic, lactic and quinic acids were measured by GC-MS and NMR. (A, chemistry only, no sensory)

Author list not verified in this session.
- Cold-brew study (PMC6207714): "little correlation between the pH and TA" for hot brew coffee. pH alone is therefore a poor proxy for perceived acidity. (A, indirect) — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6207714/)
- A Córdoba et al. 2021 *Food Chemistry* study reportedly found that more mineralised water raises coffee pH. This is known only via a retailer blog, unverified. (D) — [Colipse Coffee blog](https://colipsecoffee.com/blogs/coffee/acidity)
- A 2023 Chinese cold-brew study found that water with "moderate ion contents" gave better nutty, roasted and sweet flavours. The extract does not give alkalinity values. (B) — [Food Industry Science & Technology, doi 10.13386/j.issn1002-0306.2023050076](https://spgykj.com/en/article/doi/10.13386/j.issn1002-0306.2023050076)
- Hendon, Colonna-Dashwood & Colonna-Dashwood 2014, *J. Agric. Food Chem.*: a density-functional-theory model of Na+, Mg2+ and Ca2+ binding to coffee acids, caffeine and eugenol. It addresses cations, not bicarbonate. Later secondary commentary says bicarbonate's role is acid neutralisation, which "in addition to lowering the perceived acidity, increases the brewing time." (B) — [Bath research portal](https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/the-role-of-dissolved-cations-in-coffee-extraction/); [Daily Coffee News](https://dailycoffeenews.com/2014/06/02/uk-barista-champion-maxwell-colonna-dashwood-diving-deep-into-water-science)
- Five Senses notes that the Hendon model "doesn't predict big differences in the flavour of the brew, so much as in the overall extraction." (D) — [Five Senses](https://fivesenses.com.au/blogs/news/experimenting-with-the-effect-of-water-quality-on-coffee)

**Unverified numbers**

This appeared in a search extract attributed to "an older review." I could not confirm it in any source I could identify, and a dedicated search found nothing. Treat as unverified (D).

### Inferences
- **Mechanism hierarchy (consistent across A and B sources):** bicarbonate acts mainly after extraction, by neutralising titratable acids (HCO3- + H+ → CO2 + H2O). It does not mainly change which acids are extracted. The Heliyon study supports this: acid extraction was largely independent of water cations. The ACS work shows perceived sourness tracks TA, which bicarbonate consumes. For the website, alkalinity is therefore the primary predictor of acidity, fruit, brightness and florals (more alkalinity means less of each, and more flat, roasty and bitter).
- **Magnitude anchoring.** The only peer-reviewed dose ceiling is 300 mg/L HCO3- (≈ 246 mg/L as CaCO3, ≈ 14 dKH) in the Copenhagen study, where the effect was "substantial." The SCA target of about 40 mg/L as CaCO3 is roughly one-sixth of that. Common European hard tap water at 200–300 mg/L as CaCO3 sits at or above the level the Copenhagen study found clearly damaging.
- **Pangborn's ~750 ppm and Navarini's 1000 ppm are extreme.** They establish direction, not realistic thresholds. It is also unclear whether "750 ppm NaHCO3" means the salt or the ion. If the salt, HCO3- ≈ 545 mg/L.
- **The "bitter" descriptor under high alkalinity probably mixes two things.** True bitterness may rise. Acid suppression also unmasks bitterness and roast. The Copenhagen extract says "roasted and bitter notes" increased, which fits both.

### Gaps
- No effect sizes, panel sizes, intermediate dose levels or dose-response shape for Rune et al. 2026: the full text was not reachable. We cannot say whether, for example, 50 or 100 mg/L HCO3- already differs from ultrapure water.
- No primary-text verification of Pangborn 1971 numbers or attribute scores.
- No 2015–2026 peer-reviewed study found that measures brew pH *and* TA against graded water alkalinity. The Córdoba 2021 claim is unverified.
- Sivetz's figure of 0.22 pH per 100 ppm is unverified.

## Q2. Is buffer capacity or the hardness:alkalinity ratio a better predictor than KH alone? SCA Handbook, SCAE/BWT, ZHAW target zones (incl. 2024 WoC Copenhagen)

### Takeaway
Standards bodies frame water as a two-dimensional map of total hardness against alkalinity. This is the SCAE/ZHAW Water Chart and the SCA Water Quality Handbook "core zone," with alkalinity at about 40 mg/L as CaCO3 (acceptable about 40–70/75) and hardness at about 50–175 mg/L as CaCO3. Professional panels promote a ratio heuristic: alkalinity at about 1 part to 2–3 parts hardness, or hardness:buffer about 2:1. However, the only controlled peer-reviewed test (Copenhagen 2026) found alkalinity alone drove sensory change and hardness mattered little. No study tests whether the ratio predicts better than absolute alkalinity.

ZHAW's Marco Wellinger argues the relevant quantity is *alkalinity per gram of coffee*, so it scales with brew ratio. The 2024 WoC Copenhagen "New Target Zones for Water" talk exists, but its contents could not be retrieved.

### Cited Findings

### Inferences
- **Best-supported predictor for acidity outcomes:** absolute alkalinity, normalised to the amount of coffee (Wellinger's beverage-ratio argument). Total buffer delivered to the coffee bed equals alkalinity × water mass, and a given dose of coffee releases a roughly fixed amount of titratable acid.
- **The hardness:alkalinity ratio** captures two separate things:
  - acid suppression, from alkalinity;
  - extraction support and possibly sweetness and body, from Ca/Mg.

  The panels' preference for 1:2–1:3 fits "keep alkalinity low and hardness moderate." However, the Copenhagen data suggest the hardness half of the ratio adds little to sensory prediction within realistic ranges. A reasonable model uses alkalinity as the primary axis and hardness as a secondary axis (Mg-heavy hardness may slightly reduce bitterness and astringency per BWT; this is C-grade only).
- **For a prediction website:** model "acidity suppression" as a function of alkalinity (mg/L as CaCO3) × water volume ÷ coffee dose. Treat the ratio as a heuristic (grade C), not a validated predictor.

### Gaps
- The ZHAW 2024 "New Target Zones" content (actual zone boundaries, and whether they are separate for filter and espresso) was not retrievable. The ZHAW site DNS was blocked and no secondary reports were found. The BWT–ZHAW water handbook was not found.
- No peer-reviewed comparison of ratio against absolute alkalinity as a predictor was found.
- The exact current SCA Handbook numbers were not verified from an official SCA copy.

## Q3. Chloride vs sulfate: coffee-specific evidence on flavour; chloride corrosion limits

### Takeaway
I found **no controlled, peer-reviewed coffee study** comparing chloride with sulfate. The claims "chloride = sweeter/rounder, sulfate = drier/more bitter finish" come from beer brewing (the sulfate:chloride ratio for hop vs malt emphasis) and from coffee blogs or anecdotes (grade D). The only peer-reviewed coffee data involving chloride salts are:
- Pangborn 1971: CaCl2/MgCl2 at about 750 ppm gave sour or no-different coffee, depending on the account.
- Heliyon 2024: MgCl2/CaCl2 at drinking-water levels changed acid content little.

The SCA Handbook says only that high chloride and sulfate can make water salty and unbalance the cup. For machines, chloride is the binding constraint: 30 mg/L maximum for La Marzocco and Slayer, 5–15 mg/L for Synesso.

### Cited Findings
**Flavour**
- Beer-brewing heuristic, not coffee: SO4:Cl above 2:1 "favor[s] hop perception (dry, bitter, crisp)"; below 1:2 "favor[s] malt perception (round, full, sweet)." (Beer, C/D) — [Brewer's Friend](https://www.brewersfriend.com/2018/02/13/brewing-water-basics-putting-it-all-together); [Colorado Brewers Guild](https://coloradobeer.org/tech-safety-posts/shedding-some-light-on-water-chemistry/)
- Coffee blogs asserting the same roles (D) — [Apax Lab](https://apaxlab.com/blogs/news/apax-ion-triangle-the-role-of-ions-on-coffee); [SpecialtyBatch](https://specialtybatch.com/bottle-water-quality-coffee-uae/); [Coffee Tower](https://coffee.towerofrecords.com/coffee/water-chemistry)
  - "low concentrations of chloride ions enhance perceived sweetness."
  - "sulfate accentuates dryness, sharpens the bitter finish, and can extend the perception of astringency."
  - Chloride "useful window" 50–100 ppm, with the effect inverting above about 200 ppm.
- **Contradiction:** another guide gives a chloride target of about 25 ppm. A 50–100 ppm "sweet spot" would breach every espresso-machine chloride limit below.
- Five Senses informal tasting (D) — [Five Senses](https://fivesenses.com.au/blogs/news/experimenting-with-the-effect-of-water-quality-on-coffee)
  - MgCl2 at 100–200 ppm made coffee "sweeter, brighter, stronger and had more body" than RO water; higher levels gave "grassy, chalky" notes.
  - "the chloride and sulphate ions don't seem to have much effect on flavour at the concentrations we typically use."
- Roaster anecdote: Epsom salt (MgSO4) gave "a slight increase in perceived bitterness along with an astringent sweetness." (D) — [Grind Science](https://grindscience.com/2016/03/dissolved-minerals-in-water-and-their-effect-on-coffee/)
- SCA Handbook (per a third-party copy): chloride and sulfate "in high concentrations can make water taste salty and cause gustatory imbalance in brewed coffee." Small amounts "will increase the total hardness without affecting alkalinity" (when added as Ca/Mg salts). (C) — [pdfcoffee copy](https://pdfcoffee.com/the-water-quality-handbook-by-sca-pdf-free.html)
- Pangborn 1971 via secondary accounts: CaCl2 water gave the sourest coffee. **Contradiction:** another account reports no significant difference for CaCl2/MgCl2. (A design; B as relayed) — [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/229783358_Analysis_of_coffee_tea_and_artificially_flavored_drinks_prepared_from_mineralized_waters)

**Corrosion limits for chloride (C, manufacturer specifications)**
- La Marzocco: chloride 0–30 ppm. Its Understanding Water guide (via secondary citation) says that above 30 ppm "increases the risk of pitting corrosion." Water with "low mineral content, low pH, or high chloride content can be so corrosive (at high temperatures and pressure) that it rapidly degrades components." — [La Marzocco UK specs](https://www.lamarzocco.com/uk/en/technical-questions/la-marzocco-water-specifications/); [LM USA Water Specifications PDF](https://lamarzoccousa.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/Water-Specifications.pdf); [Moqa (D)](https://www.getmoqa.com/post/the-complete-guide-to-espresso-water-taste-and-scale)
- Slayer: "Incoming water should have no more than 30ppm chloride content." — [Slayer KB](https://slayer.zohodesk.com/portal/en/kb/articles/water-treatment-requirements)
- Synesso, figures conflict between sources:
  - S-Series technical manual: chloride 5–15 ppm. — [Synesso S-Series manual](https://ep-shopify.s3.amazonaws.com/related-documents/synesso/sseries/synesso-s-series-technical-manual.pdf)
  - Retailer ES1 table: upper limit 28 ppm, "Chloride requirements only apply to machines with steel boilers." — [Prima/ProCoffeeGear ES1](https://procoffeegear.com/products/synesso-es1)
  - Forum report: below 15 mg/L. — [Home-Barista](https://www.home-barista.com/water/yet-another-water-treatment-question-t46275.html)
- A softener vendor's material reportedly recommends chloride below 80 mg/L. This is far looser and not espresso-specific. (D) — [Home-Barista thread](https://www.home-barista.com/water/concern-about-high-chloride-water-t82213.html)
- Pitting of 316L stainless is governed jointly by temperature, potential and chloride concentration, so no single universal threshold exists. (B) — [ResearchGate: 316L pitting limits](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/287089248_Limiting_conditions_for_pitting_corrosion_of_stainless_steel_EN_14404_316L_in_terms_of_temperature_potential_and_chloride_concentration)
- Ion-exchange softeners and decarbonising cartridges do not remove chloride. RO is the usual fix for high chloride. (D) — [Home-Barista](https://www.home-barista.com/water/concern-about-high-chloride-water-t82213.html)

### Inferences
- **Website labelling:** chloride→"rounder/sweeter" and sulfate→"drier/sharper bitterness" should be labelled "hypothesis borrowed from beer brewing; untested in coffee (D)."
- **The chloride machine limit dominates any flavour use of chloride.** If a recipe uses CaCl2 or MgCl2 as the hardness source, chloride rises quickly (my arithmetic: 1 mg/L Ca from CaCl2 adds about 1.77 mg/L Cl-; 1 mg/L Mg from MgCl2 adds about 2.92 mg/L Cl-). An espresso-safe recipe must therefore source hardness mostly from sulfate or bicarbonate salts, or keep total Cl- ≤ 30 mg/L (≤ 15 for Synesso).
- **Sulfate has no published espresso-machine limit** in the specifications found. Sulfate is generally less aggressive than chloride, but very high sulfate with Ca can form gypsum scale. This is general water chemistry and was not sourced in this session.

### Gaps
- No controlled coffee sensory study of chloride vs sulfate at matched cation and alkalinity was found.
- No published sulfate limits from La Marzocco, Slayer, Synesso or Victoria Arduino were found. Victoria Arduino specifications were not found at all.
- BWT and Brita chloride guidance documents were not retrievable.

## Q4. Sodium: SCA target (10 mg/L) and evidence of saltiness or flattening

### Takeaway
The SCA(A) standard sets sodium "at or near 10 mg/L," and some summaries give 30 mg/L as a ceiling. One peer-reviewed study (Imamura 2024, *Food Sci. Technol. Res.*; grade A, small) found added sodium raised taste-sensor saltiness and reduced "clean taste." Pangborn 1971 reported slightly salty coffee with NaCl at about 750 ppm. No study quantifies "flattening" by sodium alone; flatness is better attributed to bicarbonate, often supplied as NaHCO3.

### Cited Findings
- SCAA 2009: sodium target 10 mg/L, acceptable "at or near 10 mg/L." (C) — [SCA standards summary](https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/sca-q-grader/brewing-science-water-chemistry/sca-water-standards); [SCAA standard copy](https://www.urbansaqua.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/www.urbansaqua.com-robust-ro-drinking-water-systems-water-standards.pdf)
- Some sources list an acceptable maximum of 30 mg/L: "below 30 mg/L, preferably around 10 mg/L, to avoid altering the sensory balance." (C/D) — [Gota review of 2018 SCA Handbook](https://gota.cafe/en/books/sca-water-quality-handbook); [hacecuentas](https://hacecuentas.com/en/water-mineralization-coffee)
- Imamura K. (Miyashita Ice Co.) 2024, "The clean taste of coffee depends on the sodium content of its extraction water," *Food Science and Technology Research* 30(2) (A, small) — [J-STAGE](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/fstr/30/2/30_FSTR-D-23-00129/_html/-char/en); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/377709196_The_clean_taste_of_coffee_depends_on_the_sodium_content_of_its_extraction_water)
  - Coffee brewed with melted-ice water (low Na) had improved "clean taste."
  - When sodium was added as an organic-acid salt, taste-sensor saltiness increased and clean taste decreased.
  - A review notes the human panel had only six people. — [Gota](https://gota.cafe/en/books/sca-water-quality-handbook)
  - Industry affiliation (an ice company) should be noted.
- Pangborn 1971 (secondary): coffee brewed with NaCl water "became slightly salty"; NaHCO3 gave the most bitter coffee. — [search extracts citing Pangborn](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/229783358_Analysis_of_coffee_tea_and_artificially_flavored_drinks_prepared_from_mineralized_waters)
- Hendon 2014 modelling: Na+ binds coffee compounds more weakly than Mg2+ and Ca2+, so it contributes less to extraction. (B) — [Bath portal](https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/the-role-of-dissolved-cations-in-coffee-extraction/); [Five Senses (D)](https://fivesenses.com.au/blogs/news/experimenting-with-the-effect-of-water-quality-on-coffee)
- La Marzocco cautions against sodium- and hydrogen-based ion-exchange softeners for its machines. (C) — [LM Water Specifications PDF](https://lamarzoccousa.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/Water-Specifications.pdf)
- A water-treatment patent notes that coffee shops typically prefer to remove monovalent cations such as sodium while keeping Ca and carbonate. (B/C) — [US 11633062](https://patents.google.com/patent/US11633062)

### Inferences
- Sodium-exchange softening removes Ca/Mg, but alkalinity stays as NaHCO3 and sodium rises. My arithmetic: about 0.46 mg/L Na per mg/L hardness removed as CaCO3. This is the worst case for flavour: high alkalinity (flat, muted acidity) plus no hardness (weaker extraction) plus high sodium (salty, "unclean"). It also creates corrosion and LSI problems. The website should flag softened tap water strongly.
- Using NaHCO3 as the buffer in DIY recipes adds sodium: 0.27 mg Na per mg NaHCO3; 40 mg/L alkalinity as CaCO3 from NaHCO3 adds about 18 mg/L Na. This exceeds the 10 mg/L target, which is one argument for KHCO3 as the buffer salt.

### Gaps
- No dose-response data for Na between 10 and 100 mg/L in coffee were found, and no human-panel-scale data for a "flattening" effect.

## Q5. Does brewing-water pH matter independently of alkalinity?

### Takeaway
Weak evidence says it does not, provided alkalinity is controlled. Wellinger (ZHAW/SCA) states alkalinity matters far more than pH. An Indonesian pour-over study (Fibrianto et al. 2018; grade B, conference paper) found taste and flavour unaffected by brewing-water pH from 5.5 to 9.1. Water pH has very little buffering mass: coffee's acids overwhelm it and the brew lands at about pH 5. pH matters for the machine through the LSI.

### Cited Findings
- Wellinger, SCA 25 Magazine: alkalinity "has a much larger effect on cup acidity than pH, as long as pH is near neutral and alkalinity is reasonably high"; "It is far more important to keep alkalinity within a certain range than pH." (C) — [SCA 25 Magazine](https://sca.coffee/sca-news/25/issue-9/english/water-and-coffee-acidity-how-to-adapt-your-water-for-different-extraction-methods-25-magazine-issue-9-pxjby)
- Fibrianto K., Ardianti A.D., Pradipta K. et al. 2018, "The influence of brewing water characteristic on sensory perception of pour-over local coffee," *IOP Conf. Ser. Earth Environ. Sci.* 102:012095 (B) — [IOP PDF](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1755-1315/102/1/012095/pdf); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/322828281_The_influence_of_brewing_water_characteristic_on_sensory_perception_of_pour-over_local_coffee)
  - "taste and flavour attributes were not affected by the pH of brewing water within the range of 5.5 to 9.1."
  - Different waters did affect "sweet and chocolate aroma, as well as oily mouth-feel."
- Arabica brew pH is typically 4.85–5.15. (C, patent) — [US 6054162](https://patents.justia.com/patent/6054162)
- During espresso extraction, pH evolves from 7.0–7.5 down to 5.5–5.0. — [Navarini & Rivetti extract](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/223367562_Water_quality_for_Espresso_coffee)
- Hendon: water could contain "150ppm of HCl and would certainly not taste very nice." This illustrates that ion identity and acid/base content matter, not TDS alone. (D/C quote) — [Daily Coffee News 2014](https://dailycoffeenews.com/2014/06/02/uk-barista-champion-maxwell-colonna-dashwood-diving-deep-into-water-science)
- SCA pH target 7.0 (6.5–7.5); La Marzocco 6.5–8. — [SCA summary](https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/sca-q-grader/brewing-science-water-chemistry/sca-water-standards); [LM UK specs](https://www.lamarzocco.com/uk/en/technical-questions/la-marzocco-water-specifications/)
- **Contradiction:** the La Marzocco Linea Micra manual gives a pH upper limit of 8.5 rather than 8. — [Micra manual](https://lamarzoccousa.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/MAN.31.05.1_MICRA_EN-FR_V1.154-min.pdf)
- A decarbonising (H+-form) ion-exchange cartridge lowers alkalinity but produces dissolved CO2 (carbonic acid), lowering pH. A patent claims this "significantly influences the extraction process." (B/C, patent) — [US 11633062](https://patents.justia.com/patent/11633062)

### Inferences
- Website rule: use alkalinity, not pH, for flavour prediction. Use pH only as an LSI input (machine safety) and as a sanity check. Very low pH, or high free CO2 after decarbonisation, signals corrosive water.

### Gaps
- The full Fibrianto data (how pH was adjusted and whether alkalinity co-varied) could not be checked.

## Q6. Espresso-machine constraints: LSI and other scale indices; manufacturer hardness and alkalinity limits; chloride thresholds

### Takeaway
Manufacturers converge on the following:
- TDS about 50–200 ppm;
- total hardness about 50–100 ppm as CaCO3 (La Marzocco 70–100; Slayer 2–6 gpg ≈ 34–103; Synesso 50–85);
- alkalinity about 40–100 ppm (La Marzocco 40–80; Slayer 40–100; Synesso < 100);
- pH 6.5–8 (8.5 in one La Marzocco manual);
- chloride ≤ 30 ppm (Synesso ≤ 15);
- chlorine ≤ 0.1 ppm.

The LSI target used by Barista Hustle is 0.2–0.7, and La Marzocco's calculator uses about 0.1–0.7. Slightly positive LSI is wanted: a thin carbonate film protects against corrosion. The LSI rises with temperature, so boiler water scales more than the cold-water value suggests. Brita and BWT cartridges handle this by decarbonisation plus a bypass setting chosen from the incoming carbonate hardness. Exact bypass tables were not retrievable.

### Cited Findings
**La Marzocco (C)** — [LM UK specs](https://www.lamarzocco.com/uk/en/technical-questions/la-marzocco-water-specifications/); [LM USA Water Specifications PDF](https://lamarzoccousa.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/Water-Specifications.pdf); [LM Micra manual](https://lamarzoccousa.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/MAN.31.05.1_MICRA_EN-FR_V1.154-min.pdf); [GB5 install guide](https://lamarzoccousa.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/11/GB5InstallGuide.pdf)
- Current specification: TDS 90–150 ppm; total hardness 70–100 ppm; alkalinity 40–80 ppm; chloride 0–30 ppm; pH 6.5–8; free chlorine 0–0.05 and total chlorine 0–0.1 ppm; iron 0–0.02 ppm.
- The specification PDF cautions against Na- and H-form ion-exchange softeners.
- The Linea Micra manual: warranty is void if water is outside the installation specification.
- The older GB5 guide gives total hardness 50–85, alkalinity 50–70, TDS 70–150, chloride < 30, silica ≤ 5 ppm. This is outdated.

**La Marzocco water calculator / LSI (C)**
- Uses an LSI range of about 0.1–0.7, with pH 6.5–8 and TDS 90–150. (Secondary description; page dated about 4 years old.) — [LM Water Calculator](https://techcenter.lamarzocco.com/en/watercalculator)

**Barista Hustle (C/D)** — [BH TWC 3.01 LSI lesson](https://www.baristahustle.com/lesson/twc-3-01-the-langelier-saturation-index/); [BH Water Calculator](https://www.baristahustle.com/app-archive-main/the-water-calculator/)

**Synesso (C)** — [Synesso S-Series manual](https://ep-shopify.s3.amazonaws.com/related-documents/synesso/sseries/synesso-s-series-technical-manual.pdf); [ES1 table](https://procoffeegear.com/products/synesso-es1)

- It warns that Everpure Claris and Cirqua formulator systems can damage the machine (warranty).
- The ES1 retailer table gives alkalinity 40–70 and chloride ≤ 28 ppm.

**SCA Handbook (C)**
- Alkalinity below 40 ppm as CaCO3 risks "too little buffer to prevent corrosion at boiling temperatures." — [search extract of SCA guidance](https://dailycoffeenews.com/2018/08/29/a-practical-water-guide-for-coffee-professionals-part-ii-the-sensory-data/); [Eight Ounce Coffee](https://eightouncecoffee.ca/blogs/news/practical-water-guide-for-coffee-professionals-part-1)

**Brita and BWT cartridges (C/D)**

**Other**

### Inferences
- **"Espresso-safe" envelope for the website** (intersection of manufacturer specifications):
  - total hardness about 70–100 ppm as CaCO3;
  - alkalinity about 40–80 ppm as CaCO3;
  - pH 6.5–8;
  - chloride ≤ 30 mg/L (≤ 15 for Synesso);
  - TDS about 90–150 ppm;
  - LSI at boiler temperature about 0.1–0.7.

  Low-mineral "third-wave" filter recipes typically have hardness ≤ 50 and alkalinity ≤ 20, with negative LSI. They fall below La Marzocco's and Slayer's minimums and should be flagged as corrosion-risk for machines, especially if they use chloride salts.
- **Flavour and machine needs pull in opposite directions on alkalinity.** Flavour favours low alkalinity. Machines want at least about 40 for buffering and a positive LSI, with an upper bound for scale. Wellinger's 10% argument implies espresso tolerates the 40–80 band with less acidity penalty than filter does.

### Gaps
- Victoria Arduino water specifications were not found.
- Pentair/Everpure technical documents (e.g. Claris, 4C/7CB5, Conditioner) and official Brita/BWT bypass tables were not retrievable; the domains were blocked and no search extracts were found.
- A manufacturer-published sulfate limit and a "Larson–Skold"-type corrosion index applied to espresso machines were not found.

## Q7. Do effects differ between espresso and filter, or with roast level?

### Takeaway
On espresso vs filter, the best-reasoned position (ZHAW/Wellinger; grade C, an expert argument from stoichiometry) is that espresso is about 10× less sensitive to water alkalinity, because far less water (and therefore buffer) contacts each gram of coffee. Bicarbonate also has espresso-specific physical effects: CO2 release enlarges crema but makes it less persistent and can slow flow (Navarini & Rivetti; B).

On roast, one peer-reviewed filter study (Liu et al. 2025; A) found higher-hardness water suited dark roasts and lower-hardness water suited light roasts. Royal Coffee's panel found alkalinity-heavy waters "performed at their best" on darker roasts (C). Specific alkalinity-by-roast numbers circulating online conflict and are unsourced (D).

### Cited Findings
**Espresso vs filter**
- Wellinger: for espresso, "only 10% of the alkalinity is available to buffer coffee acids compared with filter coffee." Filter-based recommendations "should be scaled up for espresso preparations according to the beverage ratio used." (C) — [SCA 25 Magazine](https://sca.coffee/sca-news/25/issue-9/english/water-and-coffee-acidity-how-to-adapt-your-water-for-different-extraction-methods-25-magazine-issue-9-pxjby)
- Navarini & Rivetti 2010 — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/223367562_Water_quality_for_Espresso_coffee); [BeanScene (D)](https://www.beanscenemag.com.au/how-minerals-modulate-taste-and-their-role-in-extraction/)
  - Bicarbonate gives a bigger initial crema but poor persistence and texture, and contributes to bed compaction. (B)
  - 1000 ppm HCO3- lengthened extraction time by up to 55%. (D, secondary)
- Royal Coffee: high bicarbonate makes the bloom or crema bigger and impedes extraction. (C) — [Daily Coffee News](https://dailycoffeenews.com/2018/08/29/a-practical-water-guide-for-coffee-professionals-part-ii-the-sensory-data/)

**Roast level**
- Liu et al. 2025: high or low hardness benefited extraction of taste compounds and expression of secondary tastes for dark or light roasts respectively. (A) — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0889157525008026); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/Delivery.cfm/f189cae0-8f86-4a1b-a8ff-a98a891a70b4-MECA.pdf?abstractid=5181791&mirid=1)
- Royal Coffee: alkalinity-only waters lacked sweetness but "performed at their best when applied to darker roast styles." (C) — [Daily Coffee News](https://dailycoffeenews.com/2018/08/29/a-practical-water-guide-for-coffee-professionals-part-ii-the-sensory-data/)
- **Contradictory roast-specific alkalinity numbers (D):**
  - One blog says light roasts "thrive with higher alkalinity levels of 55–70 ppm" (attributed to Scott Rao) and dark roasts 25–45 ppm.
  - Another says Rao recommends about 30–40 ppm for light roasts.
  - Neither cites primary data.
  - — [Coffee on Cue](https://www.coffeeoncue.com.au/blogs/how-to-make-coffee/coffee-water-alkalinity-matching-roast-levels-optimal-extraction); [Merakitech](https://www.merakitech.com/blogs/coffee-knowledge/espresso-water-alkalinity-the-buffer-that-changes-acidity)
- The Copenhagen 2026 study used a single Colombian coffee and brew method; a secondary source notes it "cannot establish an ideal alkalinity for every espresso roast, recipe, or machine." — [Merakitech](https://www.merakitech.com/blogs/coffee-knowledge/espresso-water-alkalinity-the-buffer-that-changes-acidity)

### Inferences
- **Acid-to-buffer model.** Lighter roasts have more titratable acid; darker roasts have less acid and more bitter and roast compounds. A given alkalinity therefore removes proportionally more of a dark roast's limited acidity, but the dark roast had less acidity to lose. The panel observation that alkalinity "works" on dark roasts more plausibly reflects that some acid suppression is desired there (it reduces sharpness and harshness) rather than tolerance. This is an inference, not tested.
- **Website model:** acidity suppression ∝ (alkalinity × brew water mass) ÷ (coffee mass × roast-dependent TA). Espresso (1:2–1:3) gets a lower penalty than filter (1:15–1:17) at the same water. Note that espresso machines usually see the *same* water for the boiler, so machine safety may push espresso water to higher alkalinity anyway, which the ratio argument says is acceptable.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study directly crosses graded alkalinity with roast level, or alkalinity with espresso vs filter, in a sensory design.
- The *Chemosensors* 2024 roast-acidity paper was mentioned only via a blog and is unverified.
- Liu et al. 2025 alkalinity values and effect sizes were not visible in the extracts.
